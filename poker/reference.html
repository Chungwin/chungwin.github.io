Part I

How to read this

Grid conventions, and the exploits that are here on purpose.

Start here

how to read this book

This is a complete baseline for beating NL25 6-max on GGPoker: what to play from every seat, and what to do with it after the flop. It is built for one pool of opponents, and it says so wherever it departs from theory. Three ways in:

  • Twenty minutes: read Part XI. Thirty-seven lines. Each one links back to its reasoning when you want it.
  • One evening: Parts II, IV, VII and VIII in that order — opening, the blinds, the flop as the bettor, the flop as the caller. That is most of the hands you will ever play.
  • The whole thing: front to back, then keep it open while you review sessions. The grids and the board-by-board lists are meant to be looked up, not memorised.

One warning. The charts are the default and the notes on opponents are the edge. A chart followed against a player it was not built for is a leak with a good excuse.

The opponent this book assumes

the NL25 pool, in one card

Every "the pool does X" in this book means the typical NL25 GGPoker player — not a solver, not a professional, and not the one aggressive regular at the table. The card is a model: the well-documented tendencies of micro-stakes online pools, consistent with the hands this book was built from. It is not a GG database. Treat each line as a direction to expect, not a number to trust, and check it against what you see (below).

  • Limps a lot, and limp-calls a raise with a wide, weak range. Never limp-raises without a premium.
  • Calls too much preflop — opens, 3-bets, squeezes. But folds to 3-bets from the Button and small blind, where the opens were light.
  • Folds to c-bets about half the time whatever the size — far too often against small bets, about right against big ones. When he does call, he calls light, and he rarely raises.
  • 4-bets rarely, and with a real hand. A 4-bet from an unknown is QQ+ / AK.
  • Bluffs the flop with a c-bet, barrels the turn with value, and almost never bluffs the river.
  • Calls the river with any pair, so thin value gets paid.
  • A raise means it. Check-raises, turn raises and river raises from passive players are two pair or better.
  • Does not adjust. He plays the same way against you whatever you do, which is why one baseline works.

Check it yourself. GG allows no outside trackers, so the evidence is its own Smart HUD across many opponents, and showdowns. Four things settle most of this card: fold to c-bet (the model says 50–60%), fold to 3-bet from the Button (55%+), 4-bet (under 4%, and what they show when they do), and what a river bettor turns over when called. If the players you meet run far from those, the reasoning in this book still holds and the specific frequencies need adjusting — the notes on opponents throughout say in which direction.

Words this book uses

skip if you know them
Show the glossary
  • Seats: Lojack (LJ, first to act — also called UTG at 6-max), Hijack (HJ), Cutoff (CO), Button (BTN), small blind (SB), big blind (BB).
  • Open / RFI: the first raise in a hand. Iso: raising after a limper to isolate him. 3-bet / 4-bet: the second and third raises. Squeeze: a 3-bet after an open and a call.
  • Range: every hand a player could have, given how he has played so far. Range advantage: whose range is stronger on this board. Nut advantage: who holds more of the very best hands it makes.
  • C-bet: the preflop raiser's bet on the flop. Barrel: a second (turn) or third (river) bet in a row. Float: calling a bet with a weak hand to bet later when he checks.
  • In position (IP): you act last on every street. Out of position (OOP): you act first.
  • Top pair / second pair: a pair with the highest / second-highest board card. Overpair: a pocket pair above every board card. Underpair: below the top card. Kicker: your other card, which decides ties.
  • Draws: flush draw (four to a flush, 9 outs), open-ender (8 outs to a straight), gutshot (4 outs), combo draw (flush draw plus straight draw, up to 15). Outs: cards that make your hand. Backdoor: a draw that needs both the turn and the river.
  • Dry / wet board: few draws possible / many. Rainbow: three suits on the flop. Two-tone: two of a suit. Monotone: three of a suit. Paired: two cards of the same rank.
  • Pot odds: the share of the final pot you are paying to call — the equity you need. Implied odds: the money you expect to win later if you hit. MDF: the share of hands you must keep so a bet cannot profit as a pure bluff.
  • SPR: stack-to-pot ratio — the stack behind divided by the pot. Low means the money goes in fast.
  • Value bet: a bet a worse hand calls. Bluff: a bet a better hand folds to. Semi-bluff: a bluff with a draw. Bluff-catcher: a hand that only beats bluffs.
  • Station: a player who calls and does not fold. Nit: one who plays only premiums. Reg / regular: a competent player you see often. Fish: a weak player.
  • bb: big blinds, the unit everything is measured in. At NL25 one big blind is $0.25 and 100bb is $25.

  • Raise / value (teal) — open, isolate, or 3-bet wanting action.
  • Bluff (coral) — 3-bet wanting a fold.
  • Call (blue) — flat and see a flop.
  • Fold (gray) — muck.

Deliberate deviations

read before "fixing" anything

These charts are not pure solver output. The differences below are intentional exploits for the NL25 GG pool. Written down so nobody "corrects" them back into theory and loses money.

  • Bluffs are chosen for equity, not blockers. Solver 3-bet bluffs (K2s–K5s) work because the opponent folds. This pool doesn't fold enough, so the bluff slot holds hands that flop well when called — suited wheel aces and K9s, not king-rag. Against a genuinely tight, folding regular, swap back toward pure blockers.
  • 3-bet ranges lean value-heavy. About 2.8 value hands per bluff in position, and closer to 4 from the big blind, where being out of position makes bluffs cost more when called. Correct against a pool that calls 3-bets too wide and 4-bets too rarely. This is not a balance error.
  • Ranges are ordered by hand strength, not by suit. Suitedness is worth ~2–3% equity and doesn't rescue a hand with no high card and no straight coverage: T8o beats 83s, K8o beats 72s, in every seat including the BB. The exception is suited aces and connected suited hands, which make nut flushes and disguised straights — those genuinely clear better-looking offsuit hands. If a grid ever shows suited junk defending while a reasonable offsuit hand folds, that's an error, not an exploit.
  • No limping anywhere, in any seat, including the SB. Raise or fold.

Part II

Opening

It folds to you and you're not in a blind. Raise or fold — never limp.

Why 2.5bb

every seat except the small blind

The size and the range are one decision. A 2.5bb open needs the blinds to fold about 64% of the time to show an automatic profit; a 3bb open needs 68%. Four points sounds like nothing, but the bottom third of the Button range — Q5s, K8o, 98o — only makes money because the price is cheap. Open 3bb and those hands flip from slight winners to slight losers, so a 3bb scheme has to trim the Button to roughly 40% to stay consistent. Every chart in this part is built at 2.5bb; that's what makes them correct.

What the extra half blind buys — and doesn't, at NL25. Against the blinds it buys folds. The NL25 big blind doesn't fold more to 3bb than to 2.5bb, because they weren't computing pot odds in the first place — so you pay 20% more per open and receive nothing for it. Against the fish who calls anyway it buys a bigger pot with your good hands, which is real, but you also play a bigger pot with your marginal ones, and out of position from the early seats those give it back.

Rake nudges the same way. GG takes a percentage of the pot up to a cap. The cap only helps in big pots; the small and medium pots that opens create pay the full rate, and a bigger open makes more of them. It's a nudge rather than an argument, but it points at 2.5.

Never size by hand. "3bb with a strong hand, 2.5bb to steal" tells the regs which one you have. The right form of that instinct is strong range, bigger size — choose the size for the seat, not the cards. That's why the small blind opens 3bb: out of position against one player, you want folds, and the whole range takes the bigger size.

Is 3bb a mistake? Applied consistently with a suitably tighter range, no — the EV gap is fractions of a big blind per hundred hands, and 3bb from LJ/HJ with 2.5bb from CO/BTN is a scheme plenty of winning low-stakes players use. The mistakes are 3bb with the wide Button range, and varying the size by hand strength. This doc uses one number so there's nothing to get wrong.

RFI — Lojack / UTG

~15% · raise-or-fold

Tightest seat — five players behind you. Note the suited-ace gap: ATs+ opens, A9s–A6s folds, then A5s–A2s opens again. That's not a typo. The wheel aces have straight coverage and blocker value the middling suited aces lack, and they're the hands you want to hold when someone 3-bets you.

RFI — Cutoff

~26% · raise-or-fold

Every suited ace, most suited kings, and a real block of offsuit broadways. Position starts paying off weaker hands.

Why the suited floor jumps around: a suited hand earns its place two different ways, and the chart uses whichever is stronger. High card — K7s opens on a king and the second-nut flush, with no straight potential at all. Connectivity — 97s and 86s open on straights and disguise, with no high card worth mentioning. The hands that fold are the ones with neither: Q7s, J7s, T7s and T6s are too weak to make a top pair you can stack off with, and too disconnected to make a straight. That's why the floor reads K7s, Q8s, J8s, T8s, then drops back to 97s and 86s — it isn't a typo, it's the two routes meeting in the middle.

RFI — Button

~46% · raise-or-fold

Every pair. Suited hands taper by rank as well as suit — all Ax/Kx/Qx suited, then J4s+, T5s+, 95s+, 85s+, 74s+, 64s+, 53s+, 43s. Suitedness is worth ~2–3% equity; it does not rescue a four-gapper. 83s and 92s fold.

Offsuit runs A2o+, K8o+, Q9o+, J9o+, T9o, 98o. Offsuit connectors and broadways beat bottom-tier suited gappers — T8o opens, 83s doesn't.

Isolating limpers

Someone limped in front of you. The most common spot at these stakes.

Isolating limpers

raise-or-fold · never overlimp

Someone limps and you want a bigger pot, heads-up, in position, against a player who has already announced a weak passive range. Two rules: never overlimp behind, and raise bigger than a normal open — limpers call too much, so the size is there to charge them, not to fold them out.

Sizing — start from your normal 2.5bb open, then:

SituationRaise to
1 limper, in position4bb
1 limper, out of position5bb
2 limpers, in position5bb
2 limpers, out of position6bb

Rule of thumb: +1bb per limper, +1bb if out of position. Against a limp-caller who never folds, size up further with the value hands and drop the thinnest hands from the range rather than trying to blast them off.

Lojack / Hijack  ~12%

Four or five players still behind who can wake up. Isolate tight and value-heavy — you get position over the limper, not over the field.

Cutoff  ~20%

Fewer players behind, so the suited aces fill in and the offsuit broadways start coming along.

Button  ~29%

Best case: guaranteed position all hand against a passive player. Wide — but note it's narrower than your BTN opening range. Two blinds still act behind you and the limper isn't folding, so the pure trash that opens profitably does not isolate profitably.

Part IV

Playing the blinds

Forced money, out of position, and — in the BB — closing the action. Different enough to live together.

SB — first in

~39% · raise to 3bb or fold

Folded to you with one player left. Raise or fold — no limping in the baseline. The range is the Button with two corners trimmed: you're out of position for the whole hand against a big blind who, at NL25, defends wide and rarely folds to a c-bet, so the weakest offsuit (K8o, 98o) and the suited junk that only opens on the Button (Q6s and below, J6s and below, T6s and below, the low gappers) come out. Every pair, every suited ace and king, every offsuit ace, and the offsuit nines from Q9o down all stay.

Size 3bb, not 2.5. Out of position you want the BB to fold more and the pot to be smaller when they don't. It's the one open where the bigger size is right by default.

Blinds vs a limper

use the Part III charts

Small blind vs a limper: tighten to roughly the Cutoff iso chart and size to 5bb. You'll be out of position for three streets and the BB is still behind you. Thin isolations here are where money quietly disappears.

Big blind vs a limper (someone other than the SB — for the SB, use the blind-vs-blind chart): you close the action and can check for free, so raise only hands that genuinely want a bigger pot — roughly the LJ/HJ iso chart plus the suited aces — and check everything else. Don't inflate a pot out of position with hands that were happy to see a free flop.

BB defense vs LJ / HJ

~22% · 3-bet 3% · call 19%

Same price as vs the Button, a far stronger range coming at you. Defense is call-heavy and narrow: every pair to JJ, suited aces, suited broadways to K9s / Q9s / J9s, connectors to 54s, and the offsuit broadways that aren't dominated to death — AQo down to ATo, KQo, KJo, QJo, JTo. The 3-bet is QQ+ and AK for value with A5s / A4s as the only bluffs.

What folds and why: everything with a weak kicker and no connectivity. K8s, Q8s, J8s and T7s call vs the Button because a 46% range is full of worse kings and queens; a 15–20% range isn't. Offsuit below ATo and KJo folds for the same reason — you're dominated, out of position, and the pot is small.

Read: against a Lojack or Hijack who opens 25%+, defend with the vs-Cutoff chart — a loose early opener is a Cutoff in disguise.

BB defense vs Cutoff

~37% · 3-bet 6% · call 31%

Between the two. Against a ~27% range the suited hands widen a lot — K5s+, Q6s+, J7s+, T6s+, and the gappers down to 53s / 43s — because a suited hand's flush and straight equity is real against a range that's mostly high cards. The offsuit widens only a little: A7o+, K9o+, Q9o+, J9o+, T9o, 98o. Value 3-bets start at TT and AQ; the four wheel aces are the bluffs.

That asymmetry is the whole difference between this chart and the Button one. Vs the Button you add offsuit junk because their range is junk too; vs the Cutoff their range is real, so the offsuit line barely moves while the suited line drops several rows.

Read: a Cutoff opening about 15% is an early opener — use the vs-LJ/HJ chart. A Cutoff opening 40%+ is a Button — use the vs-Button chart.

BB defense vs Button

~49% · 3-bet / call / fold

You're getting a price and closing the action, so you defend wide: every pair, suited hands down to 43s / 53s / 63s, and offsuit down to A2o, K7o, Q9o, J9o, T9o, 98o. The 3-bet is polarized — premiums for value (teal), suited wheel aces and K9s as bluffs (coral).

What folds and why: the suited junk — 72s, 83s, 92s, the low deuces. Suited doesn't rescue a hand with no straight coverage and a 7-high flush. K8o and A4o are better defends than 72s: they flop top pair against a 46% Button range, they have showdown value in check-down pots, and 72s has neither.

3-bet size: 4× their open — you're out of position. Vs 2.5bb that's 10bb; vs a 3bb open, 12bb. One size for value and bluffs.

Why ~50% and not the 55%+ solvers defend: a rake adjustment, and a choice rather than an oversight. GG's rake is heaviest in small pots and BB defense creates exactly those. The width came off the bottom of both corners — the suited junk (72s, 83s, 92s, low deuces) and the weakest offsuit (K6o and below, Q8o, J8o, 97o, 87o) — ordered by hand strength, not by suit.

Vs. a station: delete the coral entirely and move those hands into call. Bluff 3-bets need folds to exist.

Vs. a tight Button — one opening about 27% — use the vs-Cutoff chart.

SB — facing an open

3-bet or fold · 4× their open

No cold-calling. You're out of position against the opener and the BB can squeeze you off a flatted hand — a small pot, no initiative, no position, and a player behind who can raise. So both charts below have no blue at all: teal and coral 3-bet, everything else folds.

Sizing is 4× their open, since you want fewer callers. Vs 2.5bb that's 10bb ($2.50); vs a 3bb open, 12bb.

vs LJ / HJ  they open ~15–20% · you 3-bet ~6.5%

Tight, because their range is — but not as tight as a pure 3-bet range would be, because there are no calls here. The hands you'd flat in position against an early open (99, AJs, KQs, AQo) have nowhere to go from the small blind except into the 3-bet or the muck, and mucking all of them gives up too much. So: 99+ and AQs+ / AKo for value, and AQo, AJs, KQs as coral — 3-bets that fold to a 4-bet, win when they fold, and play fine when called. A5s / A4s / A3s are the pure bluffs.

Vs the Lojack specifically — a genuine 15% range, or an early opener you've seen show up tight — drop 99, AJs, KQs and A3s. That's ~5%, which is the right number against the strongest open you face.

vs CO / BTN  built vs the Button's ~46% · you 3-bet ~16%

Wide, because the Button is stealing and folds to 3-bets more than any other seat — the one spot where the doc's usual "keep the bluffs thin" default is wrong. Value runs down to 77, ATs, KJs, QJs, ATo and KJo — hands that are behind a Cutoff range but comfortably ahead of a Button range. Bluffs are the wheel aces plus A9s / A8s, K9s / KTs / QTs / JTs, T9s through 76s, and QJo: blockers and connected hands that play fine when called, which they will be. Roughly 2:1 value to bluffs.

Vs the Cutoff drop the thinnest value (77, 88, ATo, KJo, QJs) and the weaker bluffs (A9s / A8s, K9s, QJo, KTs / QTs / JTs). That's ~11%, which is where SB vs Cutoff should sit. Vs a station on the Button — someone calling 3-bets with half their range — drop the coral entirely and 3-bet only teal: bluffs need folds to exist.

The one exception: set-mining against a fish who opens wide and calls too much. That's the only time flatting the SB is right, and it needs a real hand — 99 and below, not suited broadways.

Blind vs blind

you're the BB · in position

Folded to the small blind happens about once an orbit, so this is as frequent as BB vs Button — and it plays nothing like it. Their range is wider and weaker than any open you face, you have position for the whole hand, and the price is worse (3bb open, so 2bb to call into 4). The answer to all three is the same: 3-bet far more, and call the rest wide.

vs a 3bb open  ~50% · 3-bet 13% · call 37%

The 3-bet is twice the size of any other BB chart and half of it is bluffs — the small blind folds to 3-bets more than any other seat, because they opened light and know it. Value runs down to 99, ATs, KJs, QJs, AJo, KQo. Bluffs are the wheel aces, K9s / K8s, 76s / 65s, and KJo / QJo. The calling band is the Button chart plus 87o and a few more, since you have position this time. Same junk folds: 72s, 83s, 92s, the low deuces, and offsuit below K7o.

3-bet size: 3× their open — you're in position. Vs 3bb that's 9bb ($2.25). Vs a 2.5bb open, 7.5bb.

vs a limp  raise 23% · check the rest · never fold

At NL25 half the small blinds complete rather than raise. You can see the flop for free, so the question is only raise or check: raise the hands that want a bigger pot in position — 44+, every suited ace, K8s+, Q9s+, J9s+, T9s / 98s / 87s, A8o+, KTo+, QTo+, JTo — and check everything else, including the small pairs and suited junk that are happy to flop for nothing. Raise to 4bb ($1.00). Against a limper who never folds, raise the same hands bigger, don't add thin ones.

BB vs an open and a caller

~21% · squeeze 3.5% · overcall 18%

Cutoff opens, Button flats, you're closing the action with 1.5bb to call into 6.5 — a great price into a bad structure: three-way, out of position, against a raiser and a caller. The chart is built for that spot; vs a Lojack open plus a caller, take out the weakest suited gappers.

Overcall the hands that flop big multiway and are cheap to give up: every pair to JJ, suited aces, suited broadways to K7s / Q8s / J8s / T8s, and the connectors and gappers to 43s. Offsuit almost entirely folds — AQo, AJo, KQo are the only ones, because top pair with a middling kicker into two players is exactly what loses money here. The Button's flat is capped (no premiums), so those three are usually good when they connect.

Squeeze QQ+, AK and AQs for value — the caller can't have a premium, and the opener's range is a Cutoff range. A5s / A4s are the bluffs; keep it to those two because you're asking two players to fold, not one. Size 5× the open (4× out of position, plus one for the caller): vs 2.5bb that's 12bb ($3.00). Hands that would 3-bet heads-up but aren't here — TT, JJ, AJs, AQo — overcall instead.

3-betting & facing 3-bets

Both halves of the raise-war, and what to do when it comes back at you.

In-position 3-betting

CO / BTN · 3-bet / call / fold

When you're the in-position 3-bettor, your range depends mostly on how wide the opener is — not on whether you're in the CO or on the BTN. These two grids are your references; the seat difference is a small tweak (noted below).

Sizing — as a multiple of their open:

Situation3-bet to
In position3×
Out of position4×
Any caller already in+1× per caller

Vs a 2.5bb open that's 7.5bb IP, 10bb OOP. Keep one size for the whole range — value and bluffs both — so you aren't giving away your hand with the bet. Against a player who calls 3-bets far too wide, size up with value and simply cut the bluffs rather than trying to find a size that folds them out.

vs a tight opener  LJ / UTG · ~15% · you 3-bet 4.5%

Their range is strong, so you tighten and polarize: value is JJ+/AQs/AK, bluffs are the suited wheel aces, and you flat a small band of pairs (set-mining) and suited broadways.

Read: against an early opener who folds to 3-bets more than half the time, AQo moves from call to 3-bet — it wins now, and folds to a 4-bet.

vs a wide opener  CO / HJ · ~25%+ · you 3-bet 7.1%

Their range is weaker, so value widens a lot — TT+, AJs, KQs, AQo all become value 3-bets — and you add a couple more bluffs (A9s, K9s). You can also flat more suited connectors in position.

Read: against a station, the bluff 3-bets (the wheel aces, A9s, K9s) call instead — no fold equity, and they play fine.

CO vs BTN — the seat tweak: on the Button you close the action, so you can flat a hair wider. In the Cutoff you still have players behind who can squeeze, so lean slightly more toward 3-bet-or-fold. Same chart, minor adjustment.

Squeezing — when someone has already called. Both charts above assume a single opener. Once there's an opener and a caller, tighten toward the vs-tight chart and cut the bluffs. The caller has capped their range by not 3-betting, but at NL25 they've also shown they don't fold — and a bluff needs two folds instead of one.

Size up (the table above: +1× per caller) and treat it as a mostly-value spot. The exception: squeeze wider when the caller is a passive fish and the opener is a reg who'll fold — you're isolating the fish, which is a value play, not a bluff.

Vs. a fish: cut the coral bluffs, keep the value, and flat to set-mine.

When they 4-bet. A chart that tells you what to 3-bet but not what to do next is half a plan. Facing a 4-bet at 100bb:

Stack off: QQ+, AK. Call: JJ and AQs, and only on the Button where you close the action. Fold everything else — including all the coral. The wheel aces were 3-bet to win the pot right there; once they 4-bet, that's gone and they're a 5bb loss, not a call.

The NL25 read that matters: this pool 4-bets rarely and almost always with a real hand. A 4-bet from an unknown here is worth more than a solver's mixed strategy — fold JJ and AQs too until you've seen someone 4-bet light.

Facing a 3-bet

4-bet / call / fold

You opened, someone 3-bet. Which chart applies depends on how wide you opened — a Lojack range is already strong and condensed, a Button range is mostly hands that can't continue. The second thing that matters is where the 3-bettor is: in position behind you, or in the blinds.

4-bet sizing — as a multiple of their 3-bet:

Situation4-bet to
In position2.2×
Out of position2.5×

As a multiple: 2.2× their 3-bet in position, 2.5× out of position. Vs a 7.5bb 3-bet that's roughly 17bb IP, 19bb OOP; vs a 10bb 3-bet from a blind, 22bb IP. At 100bb this leaves a stack-to-pot ratio where you're committed with the value hands — which is the point. Don't 4-bet small enough to price them in.

You opened LJ / HJ  continue 43% of your open

Your range is already tight, so the 3-bettor is representing a lot. Value is QQ+/AK — JJ is a call, not a 4-bet, because it's beaten by everything that continues against a 4-bet. Small pairs fold: set-mining needs implied odds you don't have out of position against a 3-bet size.

The NL25 adjustment that matters most. This pool 3-bets too little, and when they do it's usually a real hand. So against an unknown: drop the coral 4-bet bluffs entirely and tighten the calls. The exception is the aggressive reg who 3-bets your Button opens relentlessly. That's who the coral exists for — and against him, JJ and AQs become value 4-bets, and you widen the calls too rather than only 4-betting, since 4-betting alone lets him fold and keep printing.

Dominated offsuit broadways don't call 3-bets. KQo, AJo and ATo look playable and are the fastest way to lose a big pot to a better ace or king — against a value-heavy 3-betting range they're crushed by exactly the hands that 3-bet. They're folds in both charts by default, and only become calls in position against someone 3-betting far too wide.

Adjustments the charts assume away

sizes · stacks · limp-raises

Open sizes. Every chart is built vs a 2.5bb open. Stack depth. GG tables at NL25 carry plenty of 40–60bb stacks. Limp-raises. A limp followed by a raise over your iso is a premium at this level almost every time — QQ+ and AK, occasionally a fish with AJ. Fold everything but QQ+ / AK; don't call to see a flop.

Part VII

The flop — as the aggressor

You raised, one player called, the flop is down. Whether to bet comes first; how big comes second. Everything here is heads-up unless it says otherwise.

The flop in six lines

  1. First ask whose board it is. Yours → bet often. His → check. Range advantage sets how often, nut advantage sets how big.
  2. Dry board → bet small 25–33%, and bet often.
  3. Wet board, and it's yours → bet big 66–75%, and bet less often.
  4. Charging a draw? Raise to 3× their bet. Not 2×.
  5. Short stack behind you? Don't bluff big. Check the SPR first.
  6. Half-pot is almost never the right answer. It's the habit, not the choice.

Flop · Rule 0

The c-bet: whether, before how big

Single raised pots — you opened, someone called, nobody 3-bet. Sizing only matters once you've decided to bet at all, and that decision is the one that leaks money. Everything below is the flop.

A bet needs a job. Get called by worse. Fold out better. Or charge a draw that would otherwise see a free card. If your bet does none of those three, check. That's the entire test.

The default

Heads-up, in position, on a dry high board — bet. Small, and with most of your range. You raised preflop, so you hold more strong hands than they do, and they have to fold a lot of air. This is the profitable auto-pilot. The rest of this section is the exceptions to it.

How often, by board and position: one table, in Rule 1 — it gives the frequency and the size side by side, because the same thing decides both. The short version: on a dry high board in position, bet nearly everything, small. Everything else is lower.

Every extra opponent cuts the frequency hard. Someone almost always has a piece.

Why position lowers it at all. In position, checking is nearly free — you get a free card and act last on the turn, so checking back a medium hand costs almost nothing. Out of position you get neither, so you can't bet as wide: betting into position with a marginal hand means facing a raise you can't answer and a turn you'll have to guess at.

Six reasons to check instead

1 · The board hits their range harder

You open the button, BB calls, flop is 7♥ 6♥ 5♦. They hold 87s, 65s, 44, 66. You hold overcards and broadways. Being the preflop raiser does not transfer to every board — check the whole range and take a free card.

2 · Three or more players

Someone almost always has a piece, so there is little to fold out. Bet made hands and real draws. Check everything else.

3 · They don't fold

Against a station, a bluff has one job left and he isn't doing it. Bet for value or don't bet.

4 · Your hand wants to show down

Second pair on J♣ 9♥ 5♦, or ace-high on a board with draws. Worse hands fold, better hands call. Betting only isolates you against the hands that beat you. Check and win at showdown. (On a dry high board ace-high is the exception — there it bets small along with everything else; Rule 1 has the lists.)

5 · Monotone flop

9♥ 6♥ 2♥. You rarely hold the flush, they call with any single heart, and your fold equity is near zero. So don't bluff it. Made hands are the other story — top pair still bets small here, because a third of his one-heart hands get there for free. See Rule 1.

6 · They attack c-bets

Some NL25 regs raise small c-bets relentlessly. Against those, check back your medium hands and let them bluff into you.

In position vs out of position

In position

Bet wide, bet small

Checking is cheap — you get a free card and act last on the turn. So check back your medium hands happily, and bet a wide range small on boards that favour you.

Out of position

Bet narrower, with a plan

Checking invites a stab, and you can't take a free card. So every check needs a plan attached: check-call, check-raise, or check-fold. Decide which before you check.

The out-of-position trap: top pair with a weak kicker on J♠ 9♠ 5♥ from the small blind. You bet, get called, and now every turn card is a problem. Check-call keeps the pot small and lets them bluff their misses.

Flop · Rule 1

Size from the board, not from your hand

The most common mistake is picking a size because your hand is strong or weak. The board decides the size. Your hand only decides whether you bet.

Wet board — draws everywhere, and it's yours

Bet 66–75%

K♠ Q♠ 5♥  ·  A♠ J♠ 8♦  ·  Q♥ T♥ 7♣

Flop · the exception

When half-pot is the right answer

Rule 1 says half-pot is almost never correct. That's aimed at the reflex — reaching for it on every board without thinking. There are four spots where it's genuinely the best size.

1 · The board really is in the middle

J♣ 9♥ 5♦ or T♠ 8♦ 6♥ rainbow. Draws exist, but weak ones — gutshots, overcards, backdoors. A 33% bet lets a gutshot call cheaply. A 75% bet folds every hand that was going to pay you. Half-pot is the honest middle because the board is honestly in the middle.

2 · More than one opponent

Fold equity splits across players. Against two callers on a dry board, a 33% bet folds almost nobody — each of them only has to be right once. Half-pot is the smallest size that still asks a real question of three players; on wetter boards multiway goes bigger still.

Everything else in this part assumes one opponent. With more, you need a bigger size for the same fold equity and you bluff far less. The multiway section has the full treatment.

3 · SPR is about 3.5 and you want to stack off

Three half-pot bets, called each street, puts in exactly your whole stack — no awkward jam, no leftover few blinds on the river. This is the one worth memorising.

Flop · iso pots

C-betting against a limper

Someone limped, you raised, he called. The most common flop at NL25 after the big-blind one — and the easiest to play badly, because everything you learned about c-betting assumes an opponent who folds. This one doesn't.

1 · Bet for value, not for folds

A limp-caller's range is small pairs, weak aces, suited junk and offsuit broadways — capped, because anything good would have raised — and it does not fold to one bet. So the bluffs come out: air, ace-high on dry boards, bare draws. What is left bets bigger, because he calls anyway: one size up from the Rule 1 number on every board.

2 · When he raises, he has it

A passive player who limp-called and then raises your c-bet is not bluffing. Not on the flop, not on the turn. Fold everything except two pair or better and the draws with fifteen outs. Top pair top kicker is a fold to a limper's check-raise — this is the single biggest saving in this part.

3 · When he bets into you

A small lead ("to see where he is") is usually a weak pair or a draw: raise your top pairs and better for value, call with your draws, fold the rest. A big lead from a passive player is a strong hand. Believe it.

4 · When he calls twice

Flop bet called, turn bet called: he has a pair and he is not folding it. Do not fire a third barrel as a bluff. Do fire it for value with top pair good kicker or better — a station pays three streets with second pair, and that is where the money in these pots is.

Flop · multiway

Three or more players

Everything in this chapter so far is heads-up. Add one more player and one thing changes: someone always has something. Every rule below follows from that.

1 · Bet the top of your range, bigger, and bluff almost never

Fold equity roughly halves per extra player, so a bluff that needed one fold now needs two. Meanwhile your value hands get paid by two ranges instead of one. The answer is the same shape as the iso pot: bluffs out, thin value out, and what is left bets a size up. On a dry board that is about a third of your range; on a middling board, a fifth.

2 · "Top pair" means top pair, good kicker

Heads-up, top pair with any kicker is a value hand. Against two players it is a hand that is often already behind and rarely improves. Bet it once with a good kicker; check it with a weak one and let the others show you what they have.

3 · Draws call, they don't bet

A draw multiway is worth more than heads-up — the pot is bigger when it comes in and there are more players to pay you — but as a semi-bluff it is worth less, because it will not fold anyone. So: check or call with your draws, and bet when they arrive.

4 · A player still behind you changes everything

Facing a bet with someone yet to act, you need a hand, not a price. The "any pair, any draw, any ace" rule for small bets is heads-up only: with a player behind you, call a small bet with top pair or a real draw, and a big bet with two pair or better. Closing the action — the last to speak — you can go back to the heads-up lists.

5 · A raise multiway is the nuts

Even more than heads-up. Someone who raises into two players is not bluffing at NL25. Two pair or better continues; everything else folds, top pair included.

The flop — as the caller

You called preflop and the flop is down: from the big blind, in position, and in the 3-bet pots where the stacks are already half in.

Flop · facing a c-bet

Big blind facing a c-bet

You defended the big blind against a Cutoff or Button open — one of the charts in Part IV. The flop came, you checked, he bet. Out of position, no initiative, and a decision that comes up more often than any other postflop spot in 6-max.

The most common leak at NL25 is folding here too much. Most players fold to a c-bet well over half the time, whatever its size. Against a third-pot bet you need about 20% equity to call. A pair of fours has that. So does a gutshot. The player who folds those is paying the raiser to bet, every hand, forever.

Five rules for defending

1 · The price decides most of it

A small bet (25–33%) needs about 20% equity to call. A big bet (66–75%) needs about 30%. That gap is the difference between "any pair or any draw" and "a real hand". Read the bet size before you read your cards.

2 · His board or yours?

Same question as when you are the bettor, reversed. On a dry high board he bets nearly everything, so his bet means little — call wide, raise only with two pair or better. On a low connected board he bets only his strong hands and good draws — but you hold the straights and sets, so raise more and call less.

3 · Raise with two kinds of hands

Two pair or better, and the draws that win two ways — a pair plus a draw, a flush draw plus a straight draw, an open-ender on your board. Raise to 3× a small bet, 2.5× a big one. Nothing in between: a bare gutshot, a bare middle pair, calls or folds.

4 · Never call the flop without a plan for the turn

Out of position you cannot take a free card. If your call only makes sense when he checks the turn, it does not make sense. Every call below comes with an implicit plan: call once, and fold to a turn barrel unless something improves.

5 · The flop is where they bluff. The turn is where they don't.

The flop c-bet is the most-bluffed bet in the NL25 pool. The turn barrel is the least. Defend the flop by the rules here, then treat a second big bet as the value it almost always is.

Against an early-position opener

His range is stronger and he bets it less often, so his bet means more. Drop the weak pairs and the ace-highs from the small-bet calls, and drop middle pair from the big-bet calls. Everything else holds.

Against a station

He does not fold, so the raises that were half bluff — open-enders, bare flush draws — become calls. The value raises get bigger: 4× his bet instead of 3×. He will pay it.

Against a player who c-bets every flop

The lists above already assume he bets too much. If you have seen him fire at five flops in a row, add the ace-highs to the big-bet calls and raise your top pairs on his board — he is betting a range that cannot stand a raise.

Facing a c-bet in position

You called a Cutoff or Hijack open on the Button, or a small-blind open from the big blind. He bets from out of position and you act last. Everything above still applies — the same six boards, the same raise / call / fold logic — with one shift running through all of it: position makes calling cheaper, so you call more and raise less.

Why. Out of position, calling a draw commits you to guessing on the turn; raising takes the guess away. In position the call is the plan: if he checks the turn you see a free card or take the pot with a bet, and if he bets you still have a price. That one fact moves a handful of hands from raise to call, and a few from fold to call.

The one in-position play that has no out-of-position twin. Call a small c-bet with two overcards or an ace-high, planning to bet when he checks the turn. IP 3-bet to 7.5bb, called: pot 16.5, 92.5 behind, SPR 5.6 · BB 3-bet to 10bb, called: pot 20.5, 90 behind, SPR 4.4

1 · Decide on the flop

At SPR 4 there is no "bet the flop and see". Two bets of two-thirds pot leave about 45bb behind a 110bb pot; the river is a shove whoever makes it. So sort your hand on the flop into three piles: stacking off, one bet then done, and checking. Everything below is that sort.

2 · High boards belong to the 3-bettor, low boards to the caller

A 3-betting range is big pairs and big aces; a 3-bet-calling range is pairs, suited broadways and suited connectors. Ace-high flops hit the first. Low connected flops turn the second into sets and straights. The middle is close to even. That one line decides who bets and who calls on almost every 3-bet-pot flop.

3 · The stack-off list

Hands that get all-in on the flop or turn at SPR 4, against an unknown: sets, two pair, straights, overpairs, top pair top kicker on an ace-high board, and any draw with fifteen outs. Hands that do not, however good they look: top pair with a weak kicker, second pair, an overpair on a board with a straight already possible, and any bare eight-out draw. The pool's 3-bet ranges are strong enough that "top pair" alone is not a stack-off hand.

As the 3-bettor

You 3-bet on the Button, the Cutoff called and checked to you. Three boards, one decision each: bet or check.

Out of position as the 3-bettor — you 3-bet from the blinds and the Button called — the same lists hold with two changes. On the middling and low boards, check-call your overpairs instead of betting them: betting into position at SPR 4 with a hand that cannot stand a raise builds a pot you do not control. And the thin bluffs that bet in position after his check — a gutshot, two overcards on the middling board — check-fold, because out of position you bet before you know anything. On the ace-high board, bet everything small exactly as above. The hands that checked in position split the way every out-of-position check does: the pairs and the flush draw check-call one bet, the rest check-fold.

As the caller

You opened the Button, the big blind 3-bet, you called. He bets the flop. You have position and a range full of pairs and suited hands; he has the big cards and the initiative.

If you called a 3-bet out of position — you opened the Cutoff, the Button 3-bet — tighten every call by one notch: middle pair and the bare draws fold to a small bet (the nut flush draw, nine outs and the best overcard, still calls), and only the stack-off hands continue against a big one. You cannot see a free turn card, so a hand that was "call once and see" becomes a fold.

Part IX

The turn and the river

The flop sorts your hand. The turn is where the money goes in. The river is where you decide whether to pay. Principles, not lists — by the turn there are too many boards for lists, and a few questions do the work.

Turn · as the bettor

Barrel or give up

You bet the flop and got called. The turn card comes. One question decides most of what follows.

1 · Whose card is it?

The same question as the flop, asked again. An overcard to a middling board — a King on 9-7-4 — is yours: your range is full of Kings and his is not. A card that connects the board — an 8 on 9-7-4 — is his: his range is full of connectors and yours is not. A brick changes nothing, and the flop's answer stands. Barrel when the card is yours. Slow down when it is his.

2 · Turn bets are value or semi-bluffs, and they are bigger

By the turn his range has called once, so it is stronger, and the pot is bigger. Two things follow. First, the size: 66–75% is the default, up from the flop, because a turn bet is polarised — you have it or you are drawing to it. Second, what bets: value (top pair with a good kicker on a dry board, two pair or better on a wet one) and semi-bluffs (eight outs or more). Pure bluffs on the turn do not work at NL25. The pool calls the flop light and the turn honest, so a second barrel with nothing is paying for a fold that is not coming.

3 · A scare card is only scary if it is his

An ace on K-9-4 after you raised preflop is yours: bet it with the hands that were betting for value, and with the hands that just made a pair of aces. A third flush card when you do not hold the flush is his, whatever you have: check, and let him tell you. A card that pairs the board is usually nobody's — it changes nothing, and the flop's answer stands.

4 · With a medium hand, check the turn to get to the river

Top pair with a weak kicker, second pair with a good kicker: hands that are ahead of his bluffs and behind his value. Betting the turn folds the first and gets called by the second. Check instead, in position or out, and one of two good things happens: a cheap showdown, or a river where he has shown weakness and you can bet for value. This is "pot control".

Three normal bets in a single-raised pot

a third of your stack

Pot 5.5bb, 97.5 behind. Bet two-thirds on the flop, turn and river and get called each time: 69bb in the pot, 66bb still behind. Stacks do not go in by betting. They go in when somebody raises. So build the pot with one pair as far as the bets take it and no further — and when you hold the nuts, get a raise in on the flop or the turn, or the stacks will still be there at showdown.

In a 3-bet pot the same three bets are 100% of the stack, which is why Part VIII says decide on the flop.

Turn · as the caller

Facing a second barrel

You called the flop. He bets again. The pool is not bluffing here, and your defence changes shape to match.

1 · Fold more than you did on the flop

The minimum-defence arithmetic that made you call the flop with any pair assumes he bluffs at the right frequency. On the turn at NL25 he does not, so the arithmetic does not apply. Continue with top pair, good kicker or better; with two pair or better against a big bet on a wet board; and with draws of eight outs or more when the price is there. Second pair, weak top pair against a big bet, and gutshots fold. That is the whole list.

2 · If the turn made your hand, raise

Your draw came in, or you turned two pair. Raise, and raise now. Slow-playing against a player who has just bet twice gives up the one street where he is certain to put money in, and the river card can kill your action. At NL25 they pay a turn raise with one pair often enough that it is never wrong.

3 · If he checks the turn, he has usually given up

A player who bet the flop and checks the turn is telling you something. In position, bet: the float from Part VIII completes here, and your medium hands become value bets against a range that just admitted it missed. Out of position, lead the river with anything you would have called with — he will not bet it for you.

4 · A raise on the turn means stacks

Because of the geometry above: a turn raise is the moment the stacks become reachable. Which means a turn raise from a passive player is two pair or better, nearly always. Against it, fold one pair. Every time. The exception is the aggressive regular who raises turns as a line — and you will know him by the time it matters.

River

Value or bluff. Nothing else.

No more cards. Every bet on the river is one of two things, and if you cannot say which, you should not be betting.

1 · The two questions

Would a worse hand call? Then bet for value. Would a better hand fold? Then bet as a bluff. If the answer to both is no — a worse hand won't call and a better hand won't fold — check. That is most rivers with one pair, and there is no shame in it.

2 · Value: bet thinner than feels comfortable

The pool calls river bets with any pair. So top pair, good kicker is a river value bet against a caller, and two pair is a big one. Size 50–75%: enough to matter, small enough that a pair still calls. Overbet only with the nuts against someone who has shown he calls anything.

3 · Bluff: only when the story holds and he can fold

Two conditions, both required. The story: you have bet the hand as if you had it, and the river card does not contradict you. The opponent: someone who folds. A station does not fold the river, and a river bluff into one is the most expensive mistake in this book. When both conditions hold, bluff big — 75% or more — because a small bluff gets called by the pairs you are trying to fold. When the flop bet was your last credible bet, give up and check.

4 · Bluff-catching: does he bluff here?

Facing a river bet, the price is not the question; his range is. The pool under-bluffs the river badly. So: call small bets with top pair or better, because small river bets are often thin value from hands you beat. Fold one pair to a big river bet from a passive player without a read, whatever the price says. And a river check-raise from anyone at NL25 is the nuts — fold everything but the near-nuts.

5 · The missed draw

You bet the flop and turn with a draw and it missed. Bluffing the river is right only if rule 3 holds — the story and the opponent. Against a caller, check and lose the minimum. You called with a draw and it missed: fold, and do not turn it into a bluff-raise. That play needs a fold from a player who just bet three streets, and at NL25 he has it.

What the pool does on the turn and river, in one line: calls the flop light, barrels the turn with value, calls the river with any pair, and never bluffs it. Every rule in this part is that sentence turned around: fold more to their turn bets, value-bet thinner on their rivers, and bluff almost never.

Lessons from this part

  • The turn card: yours or his? Barrel when it's yours, slow down when it's his.
  • Turn bets are bigger and they are value or semi-bluffs. Pure bluffs on the turn don't work at NL25.
  • Facing a second barrel, fold more: second pair and weak top pair fold to a big turn bet.
  • Stacks don't go in by betting; they go in when somebody raises. A turn raise means stacks.
  • Every river bet is value or a bluff. If a worse hand can't call and a better hand won't fold, check.
  • The pool doesn't bluff the river. Call small bets with top pair; fold one pair to a big bet from a passive player.

Part X

Sizing tools for any street

Charging draws, stack-to-pot ratio, and the tables worth knowing by heart. These apply on the turn and river as much as the flop.

Any street · Rule 2

Charging a draw? Raise to 3× their bet

This one costs real money, and it's easy to get wrong because a raise feels bigger than it is.

Why 2× isn't enough: they already have their bet in the pot. If they bet $15 and you raise to $30, you are only asking them for $15 more — while the pot has grown a lot. That's a cheap call.

Pot is $20, they bet $15, you raise. Here's the equity they need to call:

to $30 18.8%
to $37.50 23.7%
to $45 27.3%
to $60 32.1%

A flush draw has 19.6% with one card to come

Raise to 2× their bet ($30) and they need 18.8% — they have more than that, so calling makes them money. Raise to 3× ($45) and they need 27.3%. Now every call loses.

On the flop they have 35% with two cards — but they only get both cards if you let them. Raise the flop, bet the turn, and they are paying the one-card price twice. The 3× rule holds on either street as long as you keep betting.

If you want the formula:

they need = (your raise − their bet) ÷ (pot + 2 × your raise)

Any street · Rule 3

Check the SPR before you bluff

SPR is just how many pots you have left behind you.

SPR = your remaining stack ÷ the pot

A small SPR is great news for your good hands — you get all-in without effort. It is bad news for your bluffs, because every bet is a huge slice of your stack on a hand you're about to fold anyway.

SPRGood handsBluffs
under 2Just get it inSize down or check
2–4Bet, bet, evaluateNormal sizing is fine
over 4Build over three streets — but stacks need a raise (Part IX)Room to apply pressure

Worked example

You squeezed preflop against shorter stacks and got one caller. Pot 28bb with 50bb behind → SPR 1.8. A half-pot bet of 14bb holding no pair is 28% of the entire stack on a hand that folds to any call. A 30% bet of 8bb does the same job for 17%.

Why small bets are so strong. Bet 25% and they have to continue with 80% of their range to stop you printing. That is genuinely hard, especially out of position — almost nobody defends that wide. It's the same reason this book keeps pushing you toward 25–33% on dry boards: it's not just cheap, it's the size they're least equipped to answer.

Two honest caveats. MDF assumes they bluff at the correct frequency. Most NL25 players under-bluff badly, so defending to MDF against a passive player just donates — fold more than the table says against anyone who doesn't bluff. And MDF is a river concept: on the flop and turn your equity and your draws matter more than the frequency. Use it as a sanity check, not a rule.

The flipside they don't use. The correct counter to a small bet is to raise it often, because a 25% bet risks little and folds a lot. Very few players at this level do. That's a leak on their side you can exploit — and one to watch for if you ever meet someone who does.

Part XI

The lessons

Everything above, compressed to what you can carry to the table. One line each; the link goes to the reasoning.

Preflop

  1. Raise or fold. Never limp, never overlimp, never cold-call from the small blind. why
  2. One open size per seat, never by hand: 2.5bb everywhere, 3bb from the small blind. why
  3. 3-bet 3× in position, 4× out of position, +1× per caller. 4-bet 2.2× / 2.5×. why
  4. Bluff 3-bets need folds to exist. Against a station, delete them. why
  5. Offsuit broadways are stronger than they feel; suited junk is weaker. T8o beats 83s. why
  6. A 4-bet at NL25 is a real hand until proven otherwise. why

The flop, as the bettor

  1. First ask whose board it is. Range advantage sets how often you bet; nut advantage sets how big. Neither means check. why
  2. A bet needs a job: called by worse, fold out better, or charge a draw. No job, no bet. why
  3. Size from the board, not from your hand. Dry: 25–33%. Wet and yours: 66–75%. why
  4. The checks are always the same two families: hands that win at showdown, and hands that can't bet twice. why
  5. Half-pot is a habit, not a choice. Four exceptions, and you should be able to name which one you're in. why
  6. Out of position, every check needs a plan attached before you make it. why
  7. Monotone boards: bluff never, bet your made hands small, always. why

Iso pots & multiway

  1. A limper doesn't fold. Bet for value, not for folds — bigger, and with fewer hands. why
  2. When a passive player raises, he has it. Fold everything but the nuts and the big draws. why
  3. Multiway, someone always has something. Bet the top of your range, bigger, and bluff almost never. why
  4. With a player still behind you, you need a hand, not a price. why

The flop, as the caller

  1. Vs a small bet, any pair, any draw and any ace calls. Vs a big bet, you need a real hand. why
  2. Raise with two pair or better and the draws that win two ways. Nothing in between. why
  3. Never call the flop without a plan for the turn. why
  4. The flop is where the pool bluffs. The turn is where it doesn't. why
  5. On his board, call. On your board, raise. why
  6. In position, draws call. Out of position, draws raise a small bet and call a big one. why
  7. The float: call a small bet with overcards in position, and bet the turn when he checks. why

  2. High boards belong to the 3-bettor, low boards to the caller. why
