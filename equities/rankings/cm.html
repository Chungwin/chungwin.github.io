How to read the odds — in plain words

Example: Ucore — 3×+ 13% · Plan works 30% · Gain if it works ~0.9–3.8×

  • Plan works — the chance the company's plan succeeds. Here: Ucore gets its plant running with signed customers. 30% = about 1 in 3.
  • Gain if it works — how far the share price could rise if the plan succeeds: ~0.9–3.8× = about 0.9 to 3.8 times today's price (after new shares are issued).
  • 3×+ — both steps together: the chance the share price at least triples within 3–5 years. 13% = about 1 in 8: 30% × the 28% of the range at or above 3× = 8.4, plus 5 surprise points on the other ~92% = 4.6. The surprise points stand for a triple that comes some other way (a takeover, a surprise drill hit, a hot market), and they count only for the cases the plan and the metal do not already carry to 3×. That is why a company whose whole range sits above 3× can show 3×+ a little above its Plan works odds.

Is that good? A random small mining stock has about a 1 in 10 (~10%) chance of tripling by luck alone. Above that is better than chance. And "doesn't triple" doesn't mean you lose money — it may just rise less.

What about the metal price? Plan works is about the company, not the metal. Gain if it works runs from a cautious lower metal price to today's price — it never assumes the metal goes up. 3×+ includes the chance the metal rises or falls. The columns further right show higher metal prices, but before new shares are issued, so they look bigger.

Why 3–5 years, and what "second triple" means

Why 3–5 years. The goal is a 5–10× in about 10 years. A 3× in 3–5 years needs the same pace (~26–32% a year), so it is the first leg of that journey — and a window short enough to check: the catalysts are dated, and mistakes show up quickly.

What "second triple" means. Upside list asks whether a company can triple in the next 3–5 years (the first leg). The second triple label asks what happens after that, from about year 5 to year 10: is there room, per share, for another 3×? Two 3× legs make roughly a 10×. It is not about cash: how many months of money a company has left is on the Financials tab of its tracker card.

  • Long yes — a believable path to a second 3× by about year 10: a district-scale or still-growing resource, several assets, or production that can more than double from its own cash flow.
  • Medium partly — a second leg exists, but it is capped (for example ~1.5–2×) or depends on the metal price rising.
  • Short no — the first re-rating is most of the value: a single asset, a short mine life, a takeover-type ending, a small carried stake, or a company already too big to multiply.

It is judged per share and on today's market value: a great deposit inside a company that is already priced for it can still be partly or little.

How it affects the order. Only as a tie-break: when two neighbours are within 1 point of each other and on the same side of the benchmark line, the one with more room for a second triple ranks first. A company can never pass one with clearly better odds.

Late bloomers (watch) marks companies with room for a second triple whose odds sit below the benchmark line because their big catalysts fall beyond 3–5 years. They are re-checked every Saturday and re-scored when a catalyst moves into the window.

Reading the two together. High odds + room for a second triple = a true 10× candidate. High odds + little room = a re-rating trade. Low odds + room for a second triple = a watchlist name.

How management is graded

The people are often the asset. The deposit does not move; the team decides whether it ever gets built. So every company carries a management grade from a one-off review (5 Oct 2026), refreshed every Saturday and whenever a CEO changes.

Three sub-scores, 1–5. Track record: what the CEO, chair and key executives built, sold or ran before, counting the misses as well as the hits and how many boards they sit on today. Fit: have they done this job before — same stage, same kind of deposit, same region. Candour: only from evidence, a dated promises-vs-delivery log per company and how bad news was handled. A fact line records insider ownership and pay.

The grade. A★ tier 1 — proven at this exact job, candid, invested. B solid, or unproven but clean: the default when there is little evidence either way. C real weaknesses — slipped dates, thin fit, thin candour. D a pattern of failing shareholders.

Skin in the game (1–5). How much of their own money the team has at risk: 5 a large stake bought at market and still being added to · 3 a meaningful stake, mostly founder stock or grants, no selling · 1 under about half a percent, options only, net sellers while promoting, or a team that gets paid while shareholders lose (pay large against the market cap or rising while the share price falls, selling into weakness, bonuses on missed targets, a failed say-on-pay). Cheap founder paper and a controlling shareholder's block are not skin. Getting paid while shareholders lose is a bad signal on its own; as a repeated pattern it also counts against the grade. It is a signal of the team's own conviction and never moves the odds; when two companies' odds are within 1 point and the runway is the same, the team with more skin ranks first in the upside list.

Failure is not a mark against anyone. Almost every serial winner has a dead company behind them. What counts is how a failure ended for shareholders and what the team did next: a loss owned plainly and carried alongside shareholders is neutral, sometimes a plus (perseverance, lessons paid for); a loss hidden, re-dated into a new target, or carried by shareholders while the founder was paid is the negative — and only when it repeats does it become a pattern.

How it moves the odds. Neutral by default. "Chance it works" goes up only with a good reason (a team that has done exactly this before and delivers on dates) and down only with one (a pattern of missed promises, undisclosed problems, self-dealing). Full weight for explorers and developers, half for producers, a token amount for seniors and royalty companies, where the asset base carries the firm. Every move is written down in the history. A famous name earns nothing by itself.

How to read the multiple columns

Multiple = target P/NAV × NAV at that price ÷ today's market cap (developers 0.3–0.5×; a funded, in-construction project 0.7×). Delivery value = the company's own study prices. Price columns are mineral-specific — each row's Driver line states the four price cases used (downside / base / upside / super) in that mineral's unit; they are not comparable across rows the way the copper page's US$5–8 columns are. Where no NAV exists (no resource or study) the cells say so rather than invent one. ~ = judgement or own calibration. Before dilution — which for two of these three names is the dominant variable.

Same formula as the other tabs — target P/NAV × NAV at that price ÷ market cap — with one difference: the price cases are per mineral. Nickel is quoted in US$/t (LME), uranium in US$/lb U3O8 (term matters more than spot for a 2030s project), antimony in US$/t Rotterdam metal (no exchange; assessed prices and a China–Europe spread). The four cases per row are set to that mineral's own history: a return to the pre-spike level, roughly today, the company's study deck or a policy-supported premium, and a re-run of the last squeeze.

Where there is no NAV, there is no multiple. Antimony Resources has no resource or study; the row shows the in-situ value of its exploration target and what share of it the market is paying, which is an honest number and not a target. ATHA has no NAV either — its row uses an explorer's capture of in-situ value (5% pre-resource, 10% once a resource exists) as a proxy.

Processors have no NAV. Ucore's cells show two figures — merchant NdPr feed at 70% payable, then Machine A on heavy-rich clay feed at 50% payable plus Line 1 at 60% — and its Delivery value cell shows the strategic re-rate if the plant works, which is the real upside case. Ucore separates other companies' concentrate, so its row uses EV/EBITDA (6–8×) on run-rate EBITDA of the costed 3,600 tpa build at each NdPr price. The company publishes no opex or margin, so those EBITDA figures are my illustration (22% NdPr in feed, feed at 70% payable, opex US$3–6/kg) — treat them as a shape, and note that feed terms move the result more than the price does.

Dilution is the column that isn't printed. Centaurus needs US$110–230M of equity on a US$180M market cap; Antimony Resources needs hard dollars within two quarters; Ucore needs ~US$70M for its second phase. Read every multiple here as 'before the raise that is coming'.

The odds columns on the upside list are a separate, per-share view. Plan works is the chance the row's thesis happens inside the horizon; Gain if it works is the multiple per share after the dilution the plan needs; 3×+ is the chance it works × the share of that range at or above 3×, plus, where the row says so, a stated metal tail (the price the metal must reach and the points it adds), plus a fixed surprise points for a triple that comes some other way, such as a takeover, a surprise drill hit or a hot market (5 points for developers, 3 for the producer (Paladin)). Since 9 Nov 2026 the surprise points count only for the cases the plan and the metal do not already carry to 3×, so they are never counted twice. Because of them, a company whose whole range sits above 3× can show 3×+ odds a little above its Plan works odds: it can triple without its plan working. The arithmetic is written in each row’s reason. The metal tail uses the chance that the metal ends at or above that price in 4 years, from a volatility model measured on 10 years of month-end prices, with the expected price growing 3% a year (Chung's assumption since 8 Nov 2026, not a measured trend); it understates extreme moves. Rows show it as “of which N metal”. For rare earths and antimony there is no long monthly price series, so their volatility comes from US Geological Survey annual average prices (7 and 9 yearly changes): a rough measure, likely on the low side, marked “(rough)” on those rows. Manganese ore and rutile/graphite have no usable 10-year series at all, so Element 25 and Sovereign show “metal part: n/a” and their odds are the company alone. Those three are judgement; the price columns above are arithmetic before dilution.

  3. 3×+ odds — the chance it works × the share of that range at or above 3× (metal moves included), plus, where the row says so, a stated metal tail (the price the metal must reach and the points it adds), plus a fixed surprise points for a triple that comes some other way, such as a takeover, a surprise drill hit or a hot market (5 points for developers, 3 for the producer (Paladin)). Since 9 Nov 2026 the surprise points count only for the cases the plan and the metal do not already carry to 3×, so they are never counted twice. Because of them, a company whose whole range sits above 3× can show 3×+ odds a little above its Plan works odds: it can triple without its plan working. The arithmetic is written in each row’s reason. The metal tail uses the chance that the metal ends at or above that price in 4 years, from a volatility model measured on 10 years of month-end prices, with the expected price growing 3% a year (Chung's assumption since 8 Nov 2026, not a measured trend); it understates extreme moves. Rows show it as “of which N metal”. For rare earths and antimony there is no long monthly price series, so their volatility comes from US Geological Survey annual average prices (7 and 9 yearly changes): a rough measure, likely on the low side, marked “(rough)” on those rows. Manganese ore and rutile/graphite have no usable 10-year series at all, so Element 25 and Sovereign show “metal part: n/a” and their odds are the company alone.
  4. The line — a volatile junior with no edge has about a one-in-ten (~10%) chance of tripling in four to five years; names below it don't beat that
  5. The earlier criteria — starting discount, dated trigger, dilution, size of the prize, underwritability, survivable downside — now feed these numbers instead of a weighted order.
What “works” means, company by company

“Plan works” is the end state the company reaches inside the 3–5-year horizon if its plan succeeds, with a date, and Plan works is the chance of that whole end state, not of the next step. By stage: an explorer — a resource of the size the thesis needs plus the study or partner step the market pays for; a developer without a feasibility study — a feasibility study (or a sale or partner deal at study stage) whose economics hold at the cautious price, with funding in sight; a developer with a feasibility study — built and producing at or near design within the study capex plus contingency (or, if first production falls after the horizon, a funded build under way); a producer — its stated growth or turnaround plan delivered; a royalty company — its stated growth delivered with its main assets intact; a processor — the plant running on commercial feed with binding sales. Steps on the way (a study, a partner, a resource) are not separate definitions: they sit inside the Gain if it works range. Since 7 Nov 2026 (Chung's decision).
