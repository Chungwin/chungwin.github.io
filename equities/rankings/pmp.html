How to read the odds — in plain words

Example: Heliostar — 3×+ 16% · Plan works 35% · Gain if it works ~1.5–3.5×

  • Plan works — the chance the company's plan succeeds. Here: Heliostar's Ana Paula feasibility study confirms the mine and the build is financed mostly with debt. 35% = about 1 in 3.
  • Gain if it works — how far the share price could rise if the plan succeeds: ~1.5–3.5× = about 1.5 to 3.5 times today's price (after new shares are issued).
  • 3×+ — both steps together: the chance the share price at least triples within 3–5 years. 16% = about 1 in 6: 35% × the 25% of the range at or above 3× = 8.8, plus 5 points for gold ending above ~US$4,300, plus 3 surprise points on the other ~86% = 2.6. The surprise points stand for a triple that comes some other way (a takeover, a surprise drill hit, a hot market), and they count only for the cases the plan and the metal do not already carry to 3×. That is why a company whose whole range sits above 3× can show 3×+ a little above its Plan works odds.

Is that good? A typical mid-size producer has about a 1 in 20 (~5%) chance of tripling by luck alone. Above that is better than chance. And "doesn't triple" doesn't mean you lose money — it may just rise less.

What about the metal price? Plan works is about the company, not the metal. Gain if it works runs from a cautious lower price (US$3,500 gold / US$45 silver) — or from today's spot, if that is lower — to the higher of the two: it never assumes the metal goes up. Every row was re-run on this rule at the 7 Nov weekly review, at the 6 Nov close (gold US$4,074.9, silver US$51.94; COMEX front month).

Why 3–5 years, and what "second triple" means

Why 3–5 years. The goal is a 5–10× in about 10 years. A 3× in 3–5 years needs the same pace (~26–32% a year), so it is the first leg of that journey — and a window short enough to check: the catalysts are dated, and mistakes show up quickly.

What "second triple" means. Upside list asks whether a company can triple in the next 3–5 years (the first leg). The second triple label asks what happens after that, from about year 5 to year 10: is there room, per share, for another 3×? Two 3× legs make roughly a 10×. It is not about cash: how many months of money a company has left is on the Financials tab of its tracker card.

  • Long yes — a believable path to a second 3× by about year 10: a district-scale or still-growing resource, several assets, or production that can more than double from its own cash flow.
  • Medium partly — a second leg exists, but it is capped (for example ~1.5–2×) or depends on the metal price rising.
  • Short no — the first re-rating is most of the value: a single asset, a short mine life, a takeover-type ending, a small carried stake, or a company already too big to multiply.

It is judged per share and on today's market value: a great deposit inside a company that is already priced for it can still be partly or little.

How it affects the order. Only as a tie-break: when two neighbours are within 1 point of each other and on the same side of the benchmark line, the one with more room for a second triple ranks first. A company can never pass one with clearly better odds.

Late bloomers (watch) marks companies with room for a second triple whose odds sit below the benchmark line because their big catalysts fall beyond 3–5 years. They are re-checked every Saturday and re-scored when a catalyst moves into the window.

Reading the two together. High odds + room for a second triple = a true 10× candidate. High odds + little room = a re-rating trade. Low odds + room for a second triple = a watchlist name.

How management is graded

The people are often the asset. The deposit does not move; the team decides whether it ever gets built. So every company carries a management grade from a one-off review (5 Oct 2026), refreshed every Saturday and whenever a CEO changes.

Three sub-scores, 1–5. Track record: what the CEO, chair and key executives built, sold or ran before, counting the misses as well as the hits and how many boards they sit on today. Fit: have they done this job before — same stage, same kind of deposit, same region. Candour: only from evidence, a dated promises-vs-delivery log per company and how bad news was handled. A fact line records insider ownership and pay.

The grade. A★ tier 1 — proven at this exact job, candid, invested. B solid, or unproven but clean: the default when there is little evidence either way. C real weaknesses — slipped dates, thin fit, thin candour. D a pattern of failing shareholders.

Skin in the game (1–5). How much of their own money the team has at risk: 5 a large stake bought at market and still being added to · 3 a meaningful stake, mostly founder stock or grants, no selling · 1 under about half a percent, options only, net sellers while promoting, or a team that gets paid while shareholders lose (pay large against the market cap or rising while the share price falls, selling into weakness, bonuses on missed targets, a failed say-on-pay). Cheap founder paper and a controlling shareholder's block are not skin. Getting paid while shareholders lose is a bad signal on its own; as a repeated pattern it also counts against the grade. It is a signal of the team's own conviction and never moves the odds; when two companies' odds are within 1 point and the runway is the same, the team with more skin ranks first in the upside list.

Failure is not a mark against anyone. Almost every serial winner has a dead company behind them. What counts is how a failure ended for shareholders and what the team did next: a loss owned plainly and carried alongside shareholders is neutral, sometimes a plus (perseverance, lessons paid for); a loss hidden, re-dated into a new target, or carried by shareholders while the founder was paid is the negative — and only when it repeats does it become a pattern.

How it moves the odds. Neutral by default. "Chance it works" goes up only with a good reason (a team that has done exactly this before and delivers on dates) and down only with one (a pattern of missed promises, undisclosed problems, self-dealing). Full weight for explorers and developers, half for producers, a token amount for seniors and royalty companies, where the asset base carries the firm. Every move is written down in the history. A famous name earns nothing by itself.

How to read the multiple columns

Multiple = re-rated EV ÷ today's market cap, where re-rated EV = expected EBITDA at that price × a peer multiple (8–10× for seniors, 10–12× for growth mid-caps, 6–8× where the discount is jurisdictional or structural, ~20–25× for royalty and streaming companies — IAMGOLD, B2Gold, Fresnillo). Delivery value = the growth or turnaround delivered, at the 1 Oct spot (US$4,180 / US$61; on 6 Nov gold closed at US$4,074.9 and silver at US$51.94, COMEX). Price columns = the market adopts that long-term price: US$3,500 Au / US$45 Ag, US$4,500 / US$69, US$5,500 / US$85, US$6,500 / US$100, US$7,000 / US$108 (~65:1; the US$7,000 column is extrapolated from the two before it). Before buybacks, dilution and cost inflation.

Every column uses the same formula: re-rated EV ÷ today's market cap, where re-rated EV = the company's expected EBITDA at that metal price × a peer multiple (~8–10× for seniors, ~10–12× for growth mid-caps, ~6–8× where the discount is jurisdictional or structural, ~20–25× for royalty and streaming companies). Producers already have cash flow, so unlike the developer page there is no P/NAV step — the question is what the market pays for a dollar of margin at each price.

Delivery value holds metal prices at the 1 Oct spot (US$4,180 / US$61; silver closed at US$51.94 on 6 Nov) and assumes the growth or turnaround it has promised (a new mine, a cost reset, a restart) is delivered. It isolates the operational story from the metal.

The price columns (US$3,500 / 45 · 4,500 / 69 · 5,500 / 85 · 6,500 / 100 · 7,000 / 108) re-run the same formula with margin at that gold / silver pair. They are not "if gold hits 5,500 tomorrow, the stock does X": producers re-rate when the market adopts a price as its long-term view and the margin shows up in reported quarters, not on the day of a spike. Today's spot sits between the first and second columns; Q2 2026 realised prices sat near the middle one. Every column includes the growth step. They include the Delivery value step — do not add the two.

Which half of the pair? Each column is one scenario (~65:1). A gold-led name reads 3,500 / 4,500 / 5,500 / 6,500 / 7,000; a silver-led name reads 54 / 69 / 85 / 100 / 108; a balanced Au·Ag name sits between.

Why the fan-out differs by company. Margin is leveraged to price — revenue rises, costs don't — so a high-cost producer (B2Gold, Equinox) spreads wide across the columns and a lower-cost one (Hecla, Fresnillo, Pan American) spreads less. That leverage is exactly Upside list's argument and Safety list's warning.

What the numbers ignore: cost inflation that follows metal prices, buybacks, dividends, hedges and dilution. Treat the columns as arithmetic that orders the names, not as price targets.

The odds columns on the upside list are a separate, per-share view. Plan works is the chance the row's plan is delivered inside the horizon; Gain if it works is the multiple per share from a long-term price (US$3,500 / 45) to today's spot; 3×+ is the chance it works × the share of that range at or above 3×, plus, where the row says so, a stated metal tail (the price the metal must reach and the points it adds), plus a fixed surprise points for a triple that comes some other way, such as a takeover, a surprise drill hit or a hot market (1 point for companies worth US$10B or more, 3 for the rest). Since 9 Nov 2026 the surprise points count only for the cases the plan and the metal do not already carry to 3×, so they are never counted twice. Because of them, a company whose whole range sits above 3× can show 3×+ odds a little above its Plan works odds: it can triple without its plan working. The arithmetic is written in each row’s reason. The metal tail uses the chance that the metal ends at or above that price in 4 years, from a volatility model measured on 10 years of month-end prices, with the expected price growing 3% a year (Chung's assumption since 8 Nov 2026, not a measured trend); it understates extreme moves. Rows show it as “of which N metal”. For producers those odds are low by nature: a triple of a US$10B company is a US$30B company.

Example: Avino — dot at 83rd, ring at ~26th.

  • The bar is every gold (or silver) mine in the world, lined up from cheapest to run on the left to most expensive on the right. Green = the cheapest quarter, red = the most expensive quarter.
  • The middle line is halfway. Left of it = Lower half: cheaper than most of the world's mines. Right of it = Upper half.
  • The dot is the company today (2026 costs). 83rd means 83% of the world's output is cheaper to produce than Avino's.
  • The ring is where it stood in 2025, and the line shows the move. Avino went from the cheap side to the expensive side in one year. No ring = no 2025 comparison.

Why it matters: when metal prices fall, mines on the left still make money; mines on the right get squeezed first. A move from the left half to the right half with no fix in sight is a sell signal.

Upside list · the odds of a 3×

Leverage & re-rate — best odds of a 3×

For the investor who wants a producer that can multiply — through a growth project, a cost turnaround or the metal — ranked by the odds of a 3× per share inside 3–5 years. For producers those odds are low by nature.

How the odds are built
  1. Plan works if — the one thing that has to happen inside the horizon, and the chance it does (trigger dates, funding, track record)
  2. Gain if it works — the multiple per share after the dilution the plan needs; from the lower to the higher of today's spot and a cautious price (US$3,500 gold / US$45 silver), so it never assumes the metal goes up
  3. 3×+ odds — the chance it works × the share of that range at or above 3× (metal moves included), plus, where the row says so, a stated metal tail (the price the metal must reach and the points it adds), plus a fixed surprise points for a triple that comes some other way, such as a takeover, a surprise drill hit or a hot market (1 point for companies worth US$10B or more, 3 for the rest). Since 9 Nov 2026 the surprise points count only for the cases the plan and the metal do not already carry to 3×, so they are never counted twice. Because of them, a company whose whole range sits above 3× can show 3×+ odds a little above its Plan works odds: it can triple without its plan working. The arithmetic is written in each row’s reason. The metal tail uses the chance that the metal ends at or above that price in 4 years, from a volatility model measured on 10 years of month-end prices, with the expected price growing 3% a year (Chung's assumption since 8 Nov 2026, not a measured trend); it understates extreme moves. Rows show it as “of which N metal”.
  4. The line — a mid-tier producer with no edge has about a 5% chance of tripling in four to five years, a US$10B+ senior less; names below it don't beat that
  5. The earlier criteria — starting discount, dated trigger, dilution, size of the prize, underwritability, survivable downside — now feed these numbers instead of a weighted order.
What “works” means, company by company

“Plan works” is the end state the company reaches inside the 3–5-year horizon if its plan succeeds, with a date, and Plan works is the chance of that whole end state, not of the next step. By stage: an explorer — a resource of the size the thesis needs plus the study or partner step the market pays for; a developer without a feasibility study — a feasibility study (or a sale or partner deal at study stage) whose economics hold at the cautious price, with funding in sight; a developer with a feasibility study — built and producing at or near design within the study capex plus contingency (or, if first production falls after the horizon, a funded build under way); a producer — its stated growth or turnaround plan delivered; a royalty company — its stated growth delivered with its main assets intact; a processor — the plant running on commercial feed with binding sales. Steps on the way (a study, a partner, a resource) are not separate definitions: they sit inside the Gain if it works range. Since 7 Nov 2026 (Chung's decision).
