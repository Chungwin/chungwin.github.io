How to read the odds — in plain words

Example: Aya — 3×+ 13% · Works 42% · If it works ~1.2–2×

  • Works — the chance the company's plan succeeds. Here: Aya turns its new Boumadine project into a funded mine. 42% = a bit less than a coin flip.
  • If it works — how far the share price could rise if the plan succeeds: ~1.2–2× = one and a fifth to two times today's price (after new shares are issued).
  • 3×+ — both steps together: the chance the share price at least triples within 3–5 years. 13% = about 1 in 8.

Is that good? A typical mid-size producer has about a 1 in 20 (~5%) chance of tripling by luck alone. Above that is better than chance. And "doesn't triple" doesn't mean you lose money — it may just rise less.

What about the metal price? Works is about the company, not the metal. If it works runs from a cautious lower price (US$3,500 gold / US$54 silver) to today's (~US$4,180 / ~US$61) — it never assumes the metal goes up. 3×+ includes the chance the metal rises or falls. The columns further right show higher metal prices, but before new shares are issued, so they look bigger.

Why 3–5 years, and what "2nd 3×" means

Why 3–5 years. The goal is a 5–10× in about 10 years. A 3× in 3–5 years needs the same pace (~26–32% a year), so it is the first leg of that journey — and a window short enough to check: the catalysts are dated, and mistakes show up quickly.

What "2nd 3×" means. List B asks whether a company can triple in the next 3–5 years (the first leg). The 2nd-3× label asks what happens after that, from about year 5 to year 10: is there room, per share, for another 3×? Two 3× legs make roughly a 10×. It is not about cash: how many months of money a company has left is on the Financials tab of its tracker card.

  • Long yes — a believable path to a second 3× by about year 10: a district-scale or still-growing resource, several assets, or production that can more than double from its own cash flow.
  • Medium partly — a second leg exists, but it is capped (for example ~1.5–2×) or depends on the metal price rising.
  • Short no — the first re-rating is most of the value: a single asset, a short mine life, a takeover-type ending, a small carried stake, or a company already too big to multiply.

It is judged per share and on today's market value: a great deposit inside a company that is already priced for it can still be partly or little.

How it affects the order. Only as a tie-break: when two neighbours are within 2 points of each other and on the same side of the luck line, the one with more room for a 2nd 3× ranks first. A company can never pass one with clearly better odds.

Monster watch (watch) marks companies with room for a 2nd 3× whose odds sit below the luck line because their big catalysts fall beyond 3–5 years. They are re-checked every Saturday and re-scored when a catalyst moves into the window.

Reading the two together. High odds + room for a 2nd 3× = a true 10× candidate. High odds + little room = a re-rating trade. Low odds + room for a 2nd 3× = a watchlist name.

How management is graded

The people are often the asset. The deposit does not move; the team decides whether it ever gets built. So every company carries a management grade from a one-off review (5 Oct 2026), refreshed every Saturday and whenever a CEO changes.

Three sub-scores, 1–5. Track record: what the CEO, chair and key executives built, sold or ran before, counting the misses as well as the hits and how many boards they sit on today. Fit: have they done this job before — same stage, same kind of deposit, same region. Candour: only from evidence, a dated promises-vs-delivery log per company and how bad news was handled. A fact line records insider ownership and pay.

The grade. A★ tier 1 — proven at this exact job, candid, invested. B solid, or unproven but clean: the default when there is little evidence either way. C real weaknesses — slipped dates, thin fit, thin candour. D a pattern of failing shareholders.

Skin in the game (1–5). How much of their own money the team has at risk: 5 a large stake bought at market and still being added to · 3 a meaningful stake, mostly founder stock or grants, no selling · 1 under about half a percent, options only, net sellers while promoting, or a team that gets paid while shareholders lose (pay large against the market cap or rising while the share price falls, selling into weakness, bonuses on missed targets, a failed say-on-pay). Cheap founder paper and a controlling shareholder's block are not skin. Getting paid while shareholders lose is a bad signal on its own; as a repeated pattern it also counts against the grade. It is a signal of the team's own conviction and never moves the odds; when two companies' odds are within 2 points and the runway is the same, the team with more skin ranks first in list B.

Failure is not a mark against anyone. Almost every serial winner has a dead company behind them. What counts is how a failure ended for shareholders and what the team did next: a loss owned plainly and carried alongside shareholders is neutral, sometimes a plus (perseverance, lessons paid for); a loss hidden, re-dated into a new target, or carried by shareholders while the founder was paid is the negative — and only when it repeats does it become a pattern.

How it moves the odds. Neutral by default. "Chance it works" goes up only with a good reason (a team that has done exactly this before and delivers on dates) and down only with one (a pattern of missed promises, undisclosed problems, self-dealing). Full weight for explorers and developers, half for producers, a token amount for seniors and royalty companies, where the asset base carries the firm. Every move is written down in the history. A famous name earns nothing by itself.

How to read the multiple columns

Multiple = re-rated EV ÷ today's market cap, where re-rated EV = expected EBITDA at that price × a peer multiple (8–10× for seniors, 10–12× for growth mid-caps, 6–8× where the discount is jurisdictional or structural, ~20–25× for royalty and streaming companies — IAMGOLD, B2Gold, Fresnillo). Progress = the growth or turnaround delivered, at today's spot (US$4,180 / US$61). Before buybacks, dilution and cost inflation.

Every column uses the same formula: re-rated EV ÷ today's market cap, where re-rated EV = the company's expected EBITDA at that metal price × a peer multiple (~8–10× for seniors, ~10–12× for growth mid-caps, ~6–8× where the discount is jurisdictional or structural, ~20–25× for royalty and streaming companies). Producers already have cash flow, so unlike the developer page there is no P/NAV step — the question is what the market pays for a dollar of margin at each price.

Progress holds metal prices at today's spot (US$4,180 / US$61) and assumes the growth or turnaround it has promised (a new mine, a cost reset, a restart) is delivered. It isolates the operational story from the metal.

The price columns (US$3,500 / 54 · 4,500 / 69 · 5,500 / 85 · 6,500 / 100 · 7,000 / 108) re-run the same formula with margin at that gold / silver pair. They are not "if gold hits 5,500 tomorrow, the stock does X": producers re-rate when the market adopts a price as its long-term view and the margin shows up in reported quarters, not on the day of a spike. Today's spot sits between the first and second columns; Q2 2026 realised prices sat near the middle one. Every column includes the growth step. They include the Progress step — do not add the two.

Which half of the pair? Each column is one scenario (~65:1). A gold-led name reads 3,500 / 4,500 / 5,500 / 6,500 / 7,000; a silver-led name reads 54 / 69 / 85 / 100 / 108; a balanced Au·Ag name sits between.

Why the fan-out differs by company. Margin is leveraged to price — revenue rises, costs don't — so a high-cost producer (B2Gold, Equinox) spreads wide across the columns and a lower-cost one (Hecla, Fresnillo, Pan American) spreads less. That leverage is exactly List B's argument and List A's warning.

What the numbers ignore: cost inflation that follows metal prices, buybacks, dividends, hedges and dilution. Treat the columns as arithmetic that orders the names, not as price targets.

The odds columns on list B are a separate, per-share view. Works is the chance the row's plan is delivered inside the horizon; If it works is the multiple per share from a long-term price (US$3,500 / 54) to today's spot; 3×+ is the chance it works × the share of that range at or above 3×, plus a small chance of a 3× from the metal alone. For producers those odds are low by nature: a triple of a US$10B company is a US$30B company.

Ranking B · the odds of a 3×

Leverage & re-rate — best odds of a 3×

For the investor who wants a producer that can multiply — through a growth project, a cost turnaround or the metal — ranked by the odds of a 3× per share inside 3–5 years. For producers those odds are low by nature.

How the odds are built
  1. Works if — the one thing that has to happen inside the horizon, and the chance it does (trigger dates, funding, track record)
  2. If it works — the multiple per share after the dilution the plan needs; low end at US$3,500 gold / US$54 silver, high end at today's spot
  3. 3×+ odds — the chance it works × the share of that range at or above 3× (metal moves included), plus a small chance of a 3× without it
  4. The line — a mid-tier producer with no edge has about a 5% chance of tripling in four to five years, a US$10B+ senior less; names below it don't beat that
  5. The earlier criteria — starting discount, dated trigger, dilution, size of the prize, underwritability, survivable downside — now feed these numbers instead of a weighted order.

Read with care

Every figure is approximate and mixed-date; unit costs are on each company's own definition (Coeur and Aya do not publish AISC). The cost-curve bar puts them on one basis — by-product AISC per ounce of the main metal, silver-equivalent figures converted (±US$3–5/oz); its gold curve is Metals Focus / WGC Q1 2026 with estimated quartile lines, its silver curve an estimate from 18 mines' 2026 guidance. Multiples are illustrative re-rating arithmetic, not forecasts or targets.
