How to read the odds — in plain words

Example: NexGold — 3×+ 19% · Works 50% · If it works ~1.4–3.5×

  • Works — the chance the company's plan succeeds. Here: NexGold finishes its study and raises the money to build. 50% = a coin flip.
  • If it works — how far the share price could rise if the plan succeeds: ~1.4–3.5× = one and a half to three and a half times today's price (after new shares are issued).
  • 3×+ — both steps together: the chance the share price at least triples within 3–5 years. 19% = about 1 in 5: 50% × the quarter of the range at or above 3×, plus 2 points of luck and 5 points for gold ending above ~US$4,250.

Is that good? A random small mining stock has about a 1 in 10 (~10%) chance of tripling by luck alone. Above that is better than chance. And "doesn't triple" doesn't mean you lose money — it may just rise less.

What about the metal price? Works is about the company, not the metal. If it works runs from a cautious lower price (US$3,500 gold / US$54 silver) — or from today's spot, if that is lower — to the higher of the two: it never assumes the metal goes up. Every row was re-run on this rule at the 7 Nov weekly review, at the 6 Nov close (gold US$4,074.9, silver US$51.94; COMEX front month).

Why 3–5 years, and what "2nd 3×" means

Why 3–5 years. The goal is a 5–10× in about 10 years. A 3× in 3–5 years needs the same pace (~26–32% a year), so it is the first leg of that journey — and a window short enough to check: the catalysts are dated, and mistakes show up quickly.

What "2nd 3×" means. List B asks whether a company can triple in the next 3–5 years (the first leg). The 2nd-3× label asks what happens after that, from about year 5 to year 10: is there room, per share, for another 3×? Two 3× legs make roughly a 10×. It is not about cash: how many months of money a company has left is on the Financials tab of its tracker card.

  • Long yes — a believable path to a second 3× by about year 10: a district-scale or still-growing resource, several assets, or production that can more than double from its own cash flow.
  • Medium partly — a second leg exists, but it is capped (for example ~1.5–2×) or depends on the metal price rising.
  • Short no — the first re-rating is most of the value: a single asset, a short mine life, a takeover-type ending, a small carried stake, or a company already too big to multiply.

It is judged per share and on today's market value: a great deposit inside a company that is already priced for it can still be partly or little.

How it affects the order. Only as a tie-break: when two neighbours are within 1 point of each other and on the same side of the luck line, the one with more room for a 2nd 3× ranks first. A company can never pass one with clearly better odds.

Monster watch (watch) marks companies with room for a 2nd 3× whose odds sit below the luck line because their big catalysts fall beyond 3–5 years. They are re-checked every Saturday and re-scored when a catalyst moves into the window.

Reading the two together. High odds + room for a 2nd 3× = a true 10× candidate. High odds + little room = a re-rating trade. Low odds + room for a 2nd 3× = a watchlist name.

How management is graded

The people are often the asset. The deposit does not move; the team decides whether it ever gets built. So every company carries a management grade from a one-off review (5 Oct 2026), refreshed every Saturday and whenever a CEO changes.

Three sub-scores, 1–5. Track record: what the CEO, chair and key executives built, sold or ran before, counting the misses as well as the hits and how many boards they sit on today. Fit: have they done this job before — same stage, same kind of deposit, same region. Candour: only from evidence, a dated promises-vs-delivery log per company and how bad news was handled. A fact line records insider ownership and pay.

The grade. A★ tier 1 — proven at this exact job, candid, invested. B solid, or unproven but clean: the default when there is little evidence either way. C real weaknesses — slipped dates, thin fit, thin candour. D a pattern of failing shareholders.

Skin in the game (1–5). How much of their own money the team has at risk: 5 a large stake bought at market and still being added to · 3 a meaningful stake, mostly founder stock or grants, no selling · 1 under about half a percent, options only, net sellers while promoting, or a team that gets paid while shareholders lose (pay large against the market cap or rising while the share price falls, selling into weakness, bonuses on missed targets, a failed say-on-pay). Cheap founder paper and a controlling shareholder's block are not skin. Getting paid while shareholders lose is a bad signal on its own; as a repeated pattern it also counts against the grade. It is a signal of the team's own conviction and never moves the odds; when two companies' odds are within 1 point and the runway is the same, the team with more skin ranks first in list B.

Failure is not a mark against anyone. Almost every serial winner has a dead company behind them. What counts is how a failure ended for shareholders and what the team did next: a loss owned plainly and carried alongside shareholders is neutral, sometimes a plus (perseverance, lessons paid for); a loss hidden, re-dated into a new target, or carried by shareholders while the founder was paid is the negative — and only when it repeats does it become a pattern.

How it moves the odds. Neutral by default. "Chance it works" goes up only with a good reason (a team that has done exactly this before and delivers on dates) and down only with one (a pattern of missed promises, undisclosed problems, self-dealing). Full weight for explorers and developers, half for producers, a token amount for seniors and royalty companies, where the asset base carries the firm. Every move is written down in the history. A famous name earns nothing by itself.

Every column uses the same formula: target P/NAV × the project's NAV at that metal price ÷ today's market cap. The target P/NAV is what the market typically pays at the next confidence level — ~0.3–0.5× for a developer with a study, ~0.5–0.7× once permitted and funded, ~0.8–1.0× once producing — on this page 0.5–0.7× for studies whose permits are in hand or dated and financing is the next step, and 0.8–1.0× for the funded builders (GoGold, Denarius). So every multiple already assumes the company completes its next stage inside the 3–5 year horizon; the columns differ only in the metal price used for the NAV.

Progress holds the metal price at the company's own study deck. It isolates what you are paid purely for the company doing its job — permit, financing, build — with zero help from gold or silver. A stale study makes this column read low (GoGold's FS uses US$26.80 silver, so its Progress shows ~0.4–0.5×: that says the study is old, not that the stock is dear).

The price columns (US$3,500 / 54 · 4,500 / 69 · 5,500 / 85 · 6,500 / 100 · 7,000 / 108) re-run the same formula with the NAV at that gold / silver pair. They are not "if gold hits 5,500 tomorrow, the stock does X": the market never values a developer at spot, and the higher P/NAV band only arrives when the stage is actually completed. Read them as: "if this is where the market's long-term price settles over the horizon and the company gets there, today's market cap could be X×." They include the Progress step — do not add the two.

Worked example — Troilus. NPV US$3.2B at its US$3,600 deck plus ~US$0.09B of cash × 0.5–0.7 (permits dated, financing next) = US$1.6–2.3B ÷ US$0.87B market cap today = ~1.9–2.6× (Progress). At US$5,500 the NPV is ~US$6.3B × 0.5–0.7 = US$3.2–4.5B ÷ 0.87 = ~3.7–5.1×. The gap between the two columns is the gold bet; the Progress figure is the de-risking trade.

Which half of the pair? Each column is one scenario (~65:1). The row's Driver tag says which number applies: a gold-led name reads 3,500 / 4,500 / 5,500 / 6,500 / 7,000; a silver-led name reads 54 / 69 / 85 / 100 / 108; a balanced Au·Ag name sits between; Aftermath's columns are flat because its value is manganese.

Why the fan-out differs by company. NAV is leveraged to price — revenue rises, costs don't — so low-margin or low-grade projects (Troilus, Emperor) spread wide across the columns and high-margin ones (GoGold, Vizsla) spread little. The same leverage works on the downside, which is what the risk score is for.

What the numbers ignore: capex inflation that follows metal prices, higher royalties, and above all dilution — the multiple is on market cap, so the equity a build needs is issued before you see the re-rate. Treat the columns as arithmetic that orders the names, not as price targets.

The odds columns on list B are a separate, per-share view. Works is the chance the row's thesis happens inside the horizon; If it works is the multiple per share after the dilution the plan needs, from a long-term price (US$3,500 / 54) to today's spot; 3×+ is the chance it works × the share of that range at or above 3×, plus a fixed luck allowance of 2 points and, where the row says so, a stated metal tail (the price the metal must reach and the points it adds); the arithmetic is written in each row’s reason. The metal tail uses the chance that the metal ends at or above that price in 4 years, from a volatility model measured on 10 years of month-end prices, with the expected price held at today's (no assumed rise); it understates extreme moves. Rows show it as “of which N metal”. Those three are judgement; the columns above are arithmetic before dilution.

Comparables — what the market pays

tap a column to sort · tap any figure for its source

What the market pays per pound or ounce in the ground, and per dollar of study value. EV (enterprise value) is market cap less cash plus debt — what a buyer would pay for the project itself. EV per unit divides that by every pound or ounce in the resource, all categories (a resource is not a reserve: inferred ounces are far less certain). P/NAV is market cap over the study's base-case after-tax NPV: 0.1× is deep scepticism, 1.0× means the market believes the study. The deck column shows the metal prices each study assumed — most are below today's, so the NPVs are conservative in that one respect.

Ranking A · most likely to deliver

Alpha with more certainty

For the investor who wants to beat the index with a higher probability of success, and accepts a capped upside to get it.

What it ranks on
  1. De-risking — reserves, permits, financing line of sight
  2. Funded runway — can it sit through a gold slump without raising at the bottom
  3. Remaining mispricing — room to re-rate from developer (~0.3–0.5× NAV) toward producer (~0.8–1.0×) inside 3–5 years
  4. Jurisdiction — local licence more than national politics
  5. Execution credibility — team, backers, cost position
Ranking B · the odds of a 3×

Multi-bagger — best odds of a 3×

For the investor who wants a realistic path to 3×+ and accepts high risk — ranked by the odds of a 3× per share inside 3–5 years, not by the size of the dream.

How the odds are built
  1. Works if — the one thing that has to happen inside the horizon, and the chance it does (trigger dates, funding, track record)
  2. If it works — the multiple per share after the dilution the plan needs; from the lower to the higher of today's spot and a cautious price (US$3,500 gold / US$54 silver), so it never assumes the metal goes up
  3. 3×+ odds — the chance it works × the share of that range at or above 3× (metal moves included), plus a fixed luck allowance of 2 points and, where the row says so, a stated metal tail (the price the metal must reach and the points it adds); the arithmetic is written in each row’s reason. The metal tail uses the chance that the metal ends at or above that price in 4 years, from a volatility model measured on 10 years of month-end prices, with the expected price held at today's (no assumed rise); it understates extreme moves. Rows show it as “of which N metal”.
  4. The line — a volatile junior with no edge has about a one-in-ten (~10%) chance of tripling in four to five years; names below it don't beat that
  5. The earlier criteria — starting discount, dated trigger, dilution, size of the prize, underwritability, survivable downside — now feed these numbers instead of a weighted order.
What “works” means, company by company

“Works” is the end state the company reaches inside the 3–5-year horizon if its plan succeeds, with a date, and Works is the chance of that whole end state, not of the next step. By stage: an explorer — a resource of the size the thesis needs plus the study or partner step the market pays for; a developer without a feasibility study — a feasibility study (or a sale or partner deal at study stage) whose economics hold at the cautious price, with funding in sight; a developer with a feasibility study — built and producing at or near design within the study capex plus contingency (or, if first production falls after the horizon, a funded build under way); a producer — its stated growth or turnaround plan delivered; a royalty company — its stated growth delivered with its main assets intact; a processor — the plant running on commercial feed with binding sales. Steps on the way (a study, a partner, a resource) are not separate definitions: they sit inside the If it works range. Since 7 Nov 2026 (owner's decision).
