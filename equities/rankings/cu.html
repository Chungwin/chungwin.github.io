How to read the odds — in plain words

Example: Hot Chili — 3×+ 21% · Works 41% · If it works 2–5.5×

  • Works — the chance the company's plan succeeds. Here: Hot Chili finds a partner and the money to build its mine. 41% = a bit less than a coin flip.
  • If it works — how far the share price could rise if the plan succeeds: 2–5.5× = two to five and a half times today's price (after new shares are issued).
  • 3×+ — both steps together: the chance the share price at least triples within 3–5 years. 21% = about 1 in 5.

Is that good? A random small mining stock has about a 1 in 10 (~10%) chance of tripling by luck alone. Above that is better than chance. And "doesn't triple" doesn't mean you lose money — it may just rise less.

What about the metal price? Works is about the company, not the metal. If it works runs from a cautious lower price (US$5 copper) to today's (~US$6.5) — it never assumes the metal goes up. 3×+ includes the chance the metal rises or falls. The columns further right show higher metal prices, but before new shares are issued, so they look bigger.

Why 3–5 years, and what "2nd 3×" means

Why 3–5 years. The goal is a 5–10× in about 10 years. A 3× in 3–5 years needs the same pace (~26–32% a year), so it is the first leg of that journey — and a window short enough to check: the catalysts are dated, and mistakes show up quickly.

What "2nd 3×" means. List B asks whether a company can triple in the next 3–5 years (the first leg). The 2nd-3× label asks what happens after that, from about year 5 to year 10: is there room, per share, for another 3×? Two 3× legs make roughly a 10×. It is not about cash: how many months of money a company has left is on the Financials tab of its tracker card.

  • Long yes — a believable path to a second 3× by about year 10: a district-scale or still-growing resource, several assets, or production that can more than double from its own cash flow.
  • Medium partly — a second leg exists, but it is capped (for example ~1.5–2×) or depends on the metal price rising.
  • Short no — the first re-rating is most of the value: a single asset, a short mine life, a takeover-type ending, a small carried stake, or a company already too big to multiply.

It is judged per share and on today's market value: a great deposit inside a company that is already priced for it can still be partly or little.

How it affects the order. Only as a tie-break: when two neighbours are within 1 point of each other and on the same side of the luck line, the one with more room for a 2nd 3× ranks first. A company can never pass one with clearly better odds.

Monster watch (watch) marks companies with room for a 2nd 3× whose odds sit below the luck line because their big catalysts fall beyond 3–5 years. They are re-checked every Saturday and re-scored when a catalyst moves into the window.

Reading the two together. High odds + room for a 2nd 3× = a true 10× candidate. High odds + little room = a re-rating trade. Low odds + room for a 2nd 3× = a watchlist name.

How management is graded

The people are often the asset. The deposit does not move; the team decides whether it ever gets built. So every company carries a management grade from a one-off review (5 Oct 2026), refreshed every Saturday and whenever a CEO changes.

Three sub-scores, 1–5. Track record: what the CEO, chair and key executives built, sold or ran before, counting the misses as well as the hits and how many boards they sit on today. Fit: have they done this job before — same stage, same kind of deposit, same region. Candour: only from evidence, a dated promises-vs-delivery log per company and how bad news was handled. A fact line records insider ownership and pay.

The grade. A★ tier 1 — proven at this exact job, candid, invested. B solid, or unproven but clean: the default when there is little evidence either way. C real weaknesses — slipped dates, thin fit, thin candour. D a pattern of failing shareholders.

Skin in the game (1–5). How much of their own money the team has at risk: 5 a large stake bought at market and still being added to · 3 a meaningful stake, mostly founder stock or grants, no selling · 1 under about half a percent, options only, net sellers while promoting, or a team that gets paid while shareholders lose (pay large against the market cap or rising while the share price falls, selling into weakness, bonuses on missed targets, a failed say-on-pay). Cheap founder paper and a controlling shareholder's block are not skin. Getting paid while shareholders lose is a bad signal on its own; as a repeated pattern it also counts against the grade. It is a signal of the team's own conviction and never moves the odds; when two companies' odds are within 1 point and the runway is the same, the team with more skin ranks first in list B.

Failure is not a mark against anyone. Almost every serial winner has a dead company behind them. What counts is how a failure ended for shareholders and what the team did next: a loss owned plainly and carried alongside shareholders is neutral, sometimes a plus (perseverance, lessons paid for); a loss hidden, re-dated into a new target, or carried by shareholders while the founder was paid is the negative — and only when it repeats does it become a pattern.

How it moves the odds. Neutral by default. "Chance it works" goes up only with a good reason (a team that has done exactly this before and delivers on dates) and down only with one (a pattern of missed promises, undisclosed problems, self-dealing). Full weight for explorers and developers, half for producers, a token amount for seniors and royalty companies, where the asset base carries the firm. Every move is written down in the history. A famous name earns nothing by itself.

How to read the multiple columns

Multiple = target P/NAV × NAV at that copper price ÷ today's market cap (0.3–0.5× for studies whose permits are not yet in motion; 0.5–0.7× where permits are in hand or dated and financing is next — Marimaca, Ivanhoe, Highland, Solaris, MUX; a 0.15–0.3× take-out band for partner-dependent projects whose permit falls at or beyond the horizon — Surge, Los Andes, Oroco). Progress = each study's own price deck. Price columns = the market adopts US$5, US$6, US$7 or US$8/lb as its long-term copper price; each row's Driver tag says how much of the value is copper — a Cu·Au name (Western, NGEx, MUX) moves less on these columns than its copper share suggests. Most study decks sit at US$3.60–4.75; spot (~US$6.5/lb at end-September 2026, LME ~US$14,400/t) sits between the US$6 and US$7 columns. Names with no NAV (Atex, Kodiak, Pecoy, Copper Giant and the explorers) carry ~ judgement columns — the value if a resource or discovery lands — which barely move with copper. ~ = peer-scaled, judgement, or (US$5 and US$8) extrapolated from the neighbouring columns. Assumes the re-rate happens inside the 3–5 year horizon. Before dilution and capex re-baselining. 3×+ and Works · if it works are the per-share odds columns described under Ranking B. On narrow screens the table scrolls sideways; the rank and name stay fixed.

Every column uses the same formula: target P/NAV × the project's NAV at that copper price ÷ today's market cap. The target P/NAV is what the market typically pays at the next confidence level — ~0.3–0.5× for a developer with a study, ~0.5–0.7× once permitted and funded, ~0.8–1.0× once producing — on this page 0.3–0.5× for studies whose permits are not yet in motion, 0.5–0.7× where permits are in hand or dated and financing is next, and a 0.15–0.3× take-out band for partner-dependent projects whose permit falls beyond the horizon. So every multiple already assumes the company completes its next stage inside the 3–5 year horizon; the columns differ only in the copper price used for the NAV.

Progress holds copper at the company's own study deck. It isolates what you are paid purely for the company doing its job — permit, financing, build — with zero help from the metal. A stale study makes this column read low: Highland's 2023 FS uses US$4.02 copper and Western's 2022 FS US$3.60, so their Progress figures say the studies are old, not that the stocks are dear. A figure below 1× (Marimaca, Ivanhoe) says the market has already paid for the study.

The price columns (US$5 · 6 · 7 · 8) re-run the same formula with the NAV at that price. They are not "if copper hits US$7 tomorrow, the stock does X": the market never values a developer at spot, and the higher P/NAV band only arrives when the stage is actually completed. Read them as: "if this is where the market's long-term price settles over the horizon and the company gets there, today's market cap could be X×." They include the Progress step — do not add the two.

Worked example — Aldebaran. PEA NPV US$2.0B at its US$4.35 deck × 80% attributable = US$1.6B × 0.3–0.5 (study stage; permits not yet in motion) = US$0.5–0.8B ÷ US$375M market cap today = ~1.3–2× (Progress). At US$6 the NPV is ~US$5.4B × 80% × 0.3–0.5 = US$1.3–2.2B ÷ 0.375 = ~3.5–6×. The gap between the two columns is the copper bet; the Progress figure is the de-risking trade.

The Driver tag. Most names here are pure copper, so the columns apply as written. Western (gold ~45% of revenue), NGEx (Cu-Au-Ag), MUX (a gold-producing parent) and Amarc (gold-weighted discovery) move less on a copper-only column than their peers — and would move on gold instead.

Why the fan-out differs by company. NAV is leveraged to price — revenue rises, costs don't — so low-margin or low-grade projects (Los Andes, Hot Chili, Oroco) spread wide across the columns and high-margin or already-priced ones (Marimaca, Ivanhoe, Atex) spread little. The same leverage works on the downside, which is what the risk score is for.

What the numbers ignore: capex inflation that follows metal prices, higher royalties, and above all dilution — the multiple is on market cap, so the equity a build needs is issued before you see the re-rate. Highland is the clearest case: its equity gap is larger than its market cap. Treat the columns as arithmetic that orders the names, not as price targets.

The odds columns on list B are a separate, per-share view. Works is the chance the row's thesis happens inside the horizon; If it works is the multiple per share after the dilution or project sell-down the plan needs, from a US$5 long-term copper price to today's ~US$6.5; 3×+ is the chance it works × the share of that range at or above 3×, plus a fixed luck allowance of 2 points and, where the row says so, a stated metal tail (the price the metal must reach and the points it adds); the arithmetic is written in each row’s reason. The metal tail uses the chance that the metal ends at or above that price in 4 years, from a volatility model measured on 10 years of month-end prices, with the expected price held at today's (no assumed rise); it understates extreme moves. Rows show it as “of which N metal”. Developers are valued off long-term prices below spot, which is why a 3.5–5.5× outcome at today's copper can still carry well under even odds of a 3×. Those three are judgement; the columns above are arithmetic before dilution.

Comparables — what the market pays

tap a column to sort · tap any figure for its source

What the market pays per pound or ounce in the ground, and per dollar of study value. EV (enterprise value) is market cap less cash plus debt — what a buyer would pay for the project itself. P/NAV is market cap over the study's base-case after-tax NPV: 0.1× is deep scepticism, 1.0× means the market believes the study. Ranking B · the odds of a 3×

Multi-bagger — best odds of a 3×

For the investor who wants a realistic path to 3×+ and accepts high risk — ranked by the odds of a 3× per share inside 3–5 years, not by the size of the dream.

How the odds are built
  1. Works if — the one thing that has to happen inside the horizon, and the chance it does (trigger dates, funding, track record)
  2. If it works — the multiple per share after the dilution the plan needs; from the lower to the higher of today's spot and a US$5 long-term copper price, so it never assumes the metal goes up
  3. 3×+ odds — the chance it works × the share of that range at or above 3× (metal moves included), plus a fixed luck allowance of 2 points and, where the row says so, a stated metal tail (the price the metal must reach and the points it adds); the arithmetic is written in each row’s reason. The metal tail uses the chance that the metal ends at or above that price in 4 years, from a volatility model measured on 10 years of month-end prices, with the expected price held at today's (no assumed rise); it understates extreme moves. Rows show it as “of which N metal”.
  4. The line — a volatile junior with no edge has about a one-in-ten (~10%) chance of tripling in four to five years; names below it don't beat that
  5. The earlier criteria — starting discount, dated trigger, dilution, size of the prize, underwritability, survivable downside — now feed these numbers instead of a weighted order.
What “works” means, company by company

“Works” is the end state the company reaches inside the 3–5-year horizon if its plan succeeds, with a date, and Works is the chance of that whole end state, not of the next step. By stage: an explorer — a resource of the size the thesis needs plus the study or partner step the market pays for; a developer without a feasibility study — a feasibility study (or a sale or partner deal at study stage) whose economics hold at the cautious price, with funding in sight; a developer with a feasibility study — built and producing at or near design within the study capex plus contingency (or, if first production falls after the horizon, a funded build under way); a producer — its stated growth or turnaround plan delivered; a royalty company — its stated growth delivered with its main assets intact; a processor — the plant running on commercial feed with binding sales.
