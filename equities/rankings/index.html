    Glossary

    Every special word on these pages, in plain English. On any page, a word with a dotted underline opens its short definition when you tap it (or move to it with the keyboard); this page has the full entries. The research is by Chung Huynh, with Claude, an AI by Anthropic, doing the nightly homework.

    Educational research, not personal financial advice.

    The desk's own words

    Safety list

    The first ranking on each tab: companies ordered by how likely they are to deliver their plan.

    It looks at how far along each company is: permits in hand, money raised, a finished study, a team that has done the job before. A high place means a safer path, not a bigger gain. The safest names are often already priced for success, so they can sit low on the upside list.

    Example A producer with a funded expansion usually ranks above an explorer that is still drilling its first holes.

    Formerly on these pages: List A, Table A, "Certainty alpha" (on the producers tab "Quality")

    Upside list

    The second ranking on each tab: the same companies ordered by their odds of tripling within 3–5 years.

    One number per company, the odds of a 3× (see that entry). When two companies are within 1 point of each other, the one with more room for a second triple ranks first, then the team with more of its own money at stake.

    Example A company with 28% odds ranks above one with 15%.

    Formerly on these pages: List B, Table B, "Multi-bagger" list

    Big winner

    A share that rises many times over, for example 5× or 10× what you paid.

    Investors often call this a "multi-bagger". The desk looks for a first 3× in 3–5 years as the first step towards a 5–10× in about 10 years.

    Formerly on these pages: multi-bagger, multibagger

    Odds of a 3×

    The desk's estimate of the chance that the share price at least triples within 3–5 years.

    It combines the chance the plan works, how much of the "gain if it works" range reaches 3× or more, a part that needs a higher metal price, and a few surprise points. Every row writes out the arithmetic.

    Example 20% means roughly a 1-in-5 chance.

    Formerly on these pages: "3×+" (the column keeps that short label)

    3× (a triple)

    Three times today's share price: a 200% gain.

    Example A share bought at C$1.00 that reaches C$3.00 has made a 3×.

    Plan works

    The chance that the company's plan succeeds inside 3–5 years.

    It is the chance of the whole end state written for that company (see "Plan works if"), not of the next small step. It is about the company, not about the metal price.

    Example 50% is about a coin flip.

    Formerly on these pages: "Works"

    Plan works if

    The end state a company must reach, with a date, for its plan to count as working.

    It depends on the stage: an explorer needs a resource of the size the thesis needs; a developer a study whose economics hold with money in sight, or a funded build; a producer its stated growth plan delivered.

    Example "Feasibility study by 2027 that holds at US$3,500 gold, with funding in sight."

    Formerly on these pages: "Works if", "works means"

    Gain if it works

    How far the share price could rise, per share, if the plan succeeds.

    It is a range, valued from a cautious metal price upwards, after any money the company still has to raise.

    Example ~1.5–3.5× means between one and a half and three and a half times today's price.

    Formerly on these pages: "If it works"

    Benchmark line

    The odds a typical small mining stock has of tripling anyway: about 10% (5% for producers).

    On the upside list a company above this line has better odds than a typical stock; below it, worse. It is a yardstick, not part of any company's odds.

    Example The tick on each odds bar marks this line.

    Formerly on these pages: luck line

    Above the line

    On the upside list: better odds than the benchmark line. On the safety list: past the safety line.

    Safety line

    The cut-off in the safety list. Above it, a company no longer depends on one big yes-or-no event.

    Formerly on these pages: certainty line, "the A line"

    Surprise points

    A few points added to the odds for a triple that comes some other way, such as a takeover or a hot market.

    1 point for companies worth US$10B or more, 3 for other producers, 5 for developers and explorers. They count only on the part of the odds that the plan and the metal do not already carry to 3×.

    Example 5 surprise points on the other 80% of cases add 4 points.

    Formerly on these pages: luck points, luck allowance, "luck"

    Metal part

    The part of the odds that needs a metal price above today's.

    It comes from one model for every metal: prices are assumed to rise 3% a year on average, with each metal's usual swings. The 3% is Chung Huynh's assumption, not a forecast.

    Second triple

    After a first triple in years 1–5, is there room to triple again by about year 10? Yes, partly or little.

    It is never about cash. "Yes" needs a believable path, such as a big or growing resource, several assets, or growth paid from cash flow.

    Formerly on these pages: "2nd 3×", runway

    Late bloomers

    Companies with room for a second triple whose big catalysts fall after year 5, so their odds look low today.

    Formerly on these pages: Monster watch

    Delivery value

    The price column that shows what the market value could become if the company simply does its job, with no help from a higher metal price.

    For developers it uses the metal prices of the company's own study; for producers today's prices. A study that is years old makes this column read low.

    Formerly on these pages: "Progress" column, "own study deck"

    Price columns

    What today's market value could become if the metal settled at each price. Arithmetic, not a forecast.

    Copper in US$ per pound, gold and silver in US$ per ounce.

    Team grade

    The management team, graded A to D on track record, fit for this exact job and candour. B is the default.

    A = proven at this exact job; C = real weaknesses; D = a pattern of failing shareholders. Only an A, C or D moves the odds, by a fixed number of points.

    Formerly on these pages: "Mgmt"

    Skin in the game

    How much of their own money the team has at risk, scored 1 to 5.

    It never moves the odds or the grade; it breaks near-ties in the upside list. Shares bought in the market count more than granted ones.

    Risk 1–5

    The chance of losing money for good, or of no re-rating, within 3–5 years. 1 is the lowest.

    Reference company

    A company worth US$30 billion or more. It is followed for what it says about the sector, and it is not ranked.

    Too big to triple in 3–5 years, so it gets no odds and no entry plan. Its results show realised prices, cost inflation, spending on new mines and appetite for buying other companies; it stays in the valuation tables and on the cost curve. The tier is reviewed once a quarter.

    Example BHP, Newmont, Rio Tinto.

    Gate

    The one hurdle a company's thesis must clear for the plan to work.

    Example A permit decision, a financing, or a study result.

    Entry plan

    The model's pre-set steps for building a position in pieces, each at a price level or after an event.

    Each step is a share of the position (for example 40%). The steps are the model's rules, not Chung's own trades, and they are research, not a recommendation.

    Entry level

    The price at or under which a step of an entry plan has its condition met.

    Formerly on these pages: buy level, buy limit

    Plan vs news

    How a new company release measures up against the lines an entry plan wrote down in advance.

    Green = on the good side of the plan's line, red = on the bad side, "not decided yet" = the deciding numbers are not out yet.

    Sell trigger

    A warning sign the desk flags when it appears: costs rising much faster than peers', heavy dilution, a jurisdiction event, insider selling, worse economics, or a delay for the second time.

    It is a flag for a closer look, not an instruction.

    Market temperature

    A monthly reading of how hot the stock market is, which sets limits on the entry plans.

    Metal dial

    A metal's own setting on the market temperature page; it governs the entry plans in that metal.

    Re-rating

    When the market starts paying more (or less) for the same company, for example after a study, a permit or financing.

    Mining and money words

    9M, H1, Q3, YTD

    9M = the first nine months of the year; H1 = the first half; Q3 = the third quarter; YTD = year to date.

    AISC (all-in sustaining cost)

    The full cost of producing one ounce (or pound), including the spending needed to keep the mine going and head-office costs, but not new projects.

    Example Gold sold at US$4,000/oz with an AISC of US$1,700/oz leaves a margin of US$2,300/oz.

    Brownfield, greenfield

    Brownfield = near an existing mine or its roads and power; greenfield = new ground with none of that.

    C1 cash cost

    The direct cost of producing a pound of copper, before sustaining spending and overhead; lower than AISC.

    Capex (capital spending)

    The money needed to build a mine (initial capex) or to keep it running (sustaining capex).

    CuEq, AuEq, AgEq, GEO (metal equivalent)

    Other metals in the ore converted into one metal at assumed prices, so grades or output can be compared. GEO = gold-equivalent ounces.

    Dilution

    New shares issued to raise money, which shrink each existing share's slice of the company.

    EBITDA

    Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation: a rough measure of the cash profit from operations.

    EIA (environmental impact assessment)

    The study and approval a government requires before a mine can be built.

    EV (enterprise value)

    Market value plus debt minus cash: roughly what buying the whole business would cost.

    EV/EBITDA divides it by a year of EBITDA; EV per ounce or pound divides it by the metal in the ground.

    FCF (free cash flow)

    Cash from operations minus capital spending: what is left for dividends, debt or growth.

    FID (final investment decision)

    The board's formal go-ahead to build a mine, usually once the money is in place.

    FS, DFS, BFS (feasibility study)

    The most detailed mine study, the one used to raise construction money. DFS = definitive, BFS = bankable.

    Grade (g/t, %)

    How much metal a tonne of rock holds: grams per tonne (g/t) for gold and silver, a percentage for copper.

    Example 1 g/t = one gram of gold in a tonne of rock.

    Heap leach

    Crushed ore is stacked on lined pads and sprinkled with a solution that dissolves the metal. Cheap to build, used for lower grades.

    Inferred, indicated, measured (M&I)

    The confidence levels of a resource estimate, from inferred (lowest) to measured (highest). M&I = measured plus indicated.

    IRR (internal rate of return)

    The yearly return a project would earn on the money spent to build it.

    JV (joint venture)

    A project owned by two or more companies together.

    LME

    The London Metal Exchange, whose daily settlement prices are the benchmark for copper and nickel.

    LOM (life of mine)

    How many years a mine plan runs, or a figure averaged over those years.

    MD&A

    Management's discussion and analysis: the report filed with each set of financial statements.

    MRE (mineral resource estimate)

    A formal estimate of how much metal is in the ground, under the reporting rules (NI 43-101 in Canada, JORC in Australia).

    Mt, kt, tpd

    Million tonnes (Mt), thousand tonnes (kt), tonnes per day (tpd, the size of a mill).

    NAV (net asset value)

    The value of a company's projects (mostly their NPVs) plus cash, minus debt.

    NdPr, TREO

    NdPr = neodymium-praseodymium oxide, the rare earths used in magnets. TREO = total rare-earth oxides.

    NPV (net present value)

    What a mine's future cash flows are worth today, after a discount for time and risk. NPV5 or NPV8 = discounted at 5% or 8% a year.

    Offtake

    A contract to buy a mine's or plant's future output.

    P/NAV (price to NAV)

    Market value divided by NAV. 0.5× means the market pays half of what the studies say the projects are worth.

    PEA (preliminary economic assessment)

    An early study of whether a mine could make money. The least certain kind of study.

    It may include inferred resources, the least certain category, so it cannot declare reserves.

    PFS (pre-feasibility study)

    A study more detailed than a PEA, using only measured and indicated resources. It can declare reserves.

    Porphyry

    A large, usually low-grade type of deposit that holds most of the world's copper.

    Price deck

    The metal prices a study or a valuation assumes.

    Reserve (P&P)

    The part of a resource a PFS or FS shows can be mined at a profit. P&P = proven and probable.

    RIGI

    Argentina's incentive regime for large investments: tax, customs and currency guarantees for big projects.

    Royalty, stream, NSR

    A royalty is a share of a mine's sales. A stream is cash paid up front for the right to buy part of the future output at a low fixed price. NSR (net smelter return) = a royalty on sales after smelting and transport.

    SAMR

    China's competition regulator, whose approval some large mergers need.

    SEDAR+, EDGAR

    The official databases where Canadian (SEDAR+) and US (EDGAR) listed companies file their reports.

    SX-EW

    Solvent extraction and electrowinning: making pure copper plates (cathode) on site from a leach solution.

    TTM (trailing twelve months)

    The last four reported quarters added together.

    Warrants

    Rights to buy new shares at a set price before a set date, often handed out with a share sale.
