How we rate a share

Cheap, fair or expensive: what each label means, how it is worked out, and two worked examples with their numbers.

In one line: we estimate what a share is likely to be worth in about four years, weigh the good and the bad outcome by their chances, and compare that with today's price.

A rating is not a buy or sell recommendation and not a price target. It answers one question: at today's price, are you paid enough for the risks you take? Educational research, not personal financial advice.

In the examples: fact a number from a filing, a price series or a company release; estimate a number the analyst worked out, with its assumption; judgement a choice the analyst made, with its reason.

The five steps

  1. What has to go right. For each company we write down one plan that would make the shares worth much more within about four years, and what it takes: a study, a permit, a partner, the money to build. We call that “the plan works”. Everything else is “the plan fails”.
  2. The chance it works. A percentage, set in the company's deep dive from the evidence: how far along the project is, what still has to happen, who has to say yes. It is an estimate, not a measurement: no one yet measures how often projects at each stage get through. Where the deep dive and the rankings page give different chances, the deep dive's counts.
  3. The value per share if it works. What investors pay today for similar companies that have already reached that stage, not what the company's own study says the mine is worth: the market rarely pays that for projects not yet built. Where “works” means a mine in production, it is valued like a producing mine.
  4. The value per share if it fails. What investors pay today for similar projects stuck at the earlier stage, less the cash the company spends meanwhile. It is never more than what the market pays for this company's own project today.
  5. New shares. Most small miners must sell new shares to pay for their plan. We count every one of them, sold at 20% below today's price, so the value is per share after dilution (dilution: the same company split into more shares).

Then: expected value = chance it works × value if it works + chance it fails × value if it fails, as a multiple of today's price.

A company whose plan has its own date three to five years away is judged over that time, at the same yearly rates. A late build is a plan that still happens, but after its date; it counts as part of “fails”, valued like success but later, and it is left out for the cheap test.

A worked example: Copper Giant (TSXV: CGNT), rated expensive

Copper Giant owns Mocoa, a large copper deposit in Colombia. It has no economic study yet.

Today. fact Close on Fri 13 Nov 2026: C$0.85. Shares: 262.5 million. Market value: C$223 million. No debt. The resource: 11,490 million pounds of copper, 81% of it in the least certain category (“Inferred”). Cash: about C$45 million estimate: the 30 Jun statements plus the August placement, less one quarter of spending.

Step 1 · What has to go right. By 2030 the licence dispute ends well and a pre-feasibility study (the second of three study stages) confirms the economics, with a miner as partner or buyer.

Step 2 · The chance. 18% judgement, the analyst's figure from the deep dive.

Step 3 · Value if it works. Companies whose copper projects have a pre-feasibility study trade at a median of 2.29 US cents per pound of copper in the ground fact: three companies, from 0.83 to 3.76 cents, the desk's table of 13 Nov 2026. Mocoa at 2.29 cents is worth US$263 million, or C$375 million. Getting there costs about C$104 million estimate: about C$25 million a year for four years. The company has about C$80 million on hand once its warrants and options are used fact: 81.8 million warrants and 8.7 million options priced below C$0.85. The C$24 million still missing is raised at C$0.68 (today's price less 20%): 35 million new shares. Shares then: 388 million, 48% more than today. Value per share: C$375 million ÷ 388 million = C$0.97, or 1.14 times today's price.

Step 4 · Value if it fails. Copper projects stuck at the first-study stage trade at a median of 1.31 cents a pound fact: five companies. That is more than the market pays for Mocoa today, 1.09 cents. A stalled project cannot be worth more than the same project with its chances intact, so we use 1.09 cents: C$178 million judgement, the rule. Four years of spending that builds nothing (about C$74 million estimate) leave a gap of about C$4 million, raised at a rescue price. Value per share: C$0.52, or 0.61 times today's price.

Step 5 · The weighted result. 0.18 × 1.14 + 0.82 × 0.61 = 0.70 times today's price. A share bought at C$0.85 would be expected to be worth about C$0.60 in four years, about -8% a year. That is below 1.1 times.

Why the market might be wrong. judgement Even if everything goes right, a share is worth about 1.14 times today's price. Reaching a pre-feasibility study roughly doubles what the market pays per pound (from 1.09 to 2.29 cents), but the C$104 million it costs and a share count that grows by about half take most of that gain. For today's price to be even fair, a successful Mocoa would have to be valued at about 6.7 cents a pound, more than any of its three closest peers. Label: expensive.

What would change it. At C$0.53 or less, with nothing else changed, the label would be fair: the same values, with more shares sold at the lower price. A first study showing a much larger value than the market expects would also change it; it is due by 30 Nov 2026.

What the rankings page would say instead. Shown, not counted. The rankings use a more generous target: about 2.3 – 6.3 times today's price if the plan works (midpoint 4.3). On that target the expected value would be 1.27 times, fair. We count the first way because it rests on prices investors actually pay today.

A second example: Hot Chili (ASX/TSXV: HCH), rated expensive

Hot Chili has a pre-feasibility study for Costa Fuego in Chile: after tax and discounted at 8% a year, worth US$1.2 billion at US$4.30 copper and about US$3.0 billion at today's US$6.17 fact, the study's own price table, extended at its last step (an estimate beyond its tested range). The build costs US$1.27 billion; the company has A$29 million of cash fact.

  • If it works (a partner or a water sale funds the build by 2028): unfunded copper projects at the feasibility stage are valued at about 0.17 times their study value at today's copper, counting the projects alone (enterprise value: market value less cash plus debt) fact: four companies, the median of the desk's table. To that we add once the money Hot Chili itself puts into the build estimate. Even with a partner and 55% debt estimate, Hot Chili must raise about A$467 million, about 1.8 times its market value of A$265 million, and its share count rises about 3.2-fold, from 202.9 million shares in issue. Value if it works: 1.23 times today's price.
  • If it fails: stuck and unfunded, it is worth no more per pound than the market pays for it today, and it must raise money at a rescue price to keep going. Value if it fails: 0.62 times.
  • Result: 0.30 × 1.23 + 0.70 × 0.62 = 0.81 times. Expensive. The written reason is the dilution: for the expected value to reach 1.1 times, a funded Costa Fuego would have to be valued at about 0.47 times its study value at today's copper, and the best of the four peers is at 0.31 times.

Things to know

  • Today's metal prices. Every rating uses the metal price of the close date: COMEX gold and silver, LME copper and nickel, the month-end uranium price. Higher or lower prices are shown beside the rating, never counted.
  • Price levels. Each rating comes with two share prices: below the first it would read cheap, above the second expensive, with the new shares re-priced at each level. Some companies have no cheap price: price alone won't make them cheap; it would take good news. The reason: the value if the project stalls is fixed at today's prices (what the market pays today for the company or for similar stalled projects), so at a much lower share price the sum would claim you make money even if everything goes wrong, which is not believable.
  • When a rating changes. We review a rating when the price crosses one of its two levels, after major company news or a dated event, and at least every three months.
  • The test is cautious. A cheap label needs more evidence than an expensive one: a written reason, a dated event and a second review. So the labels lean towards fair and expensive.
  • Try it yourself. Every company page has sliders for its main assumptions and the full calculation, starting with the calculation in brief.

The numbers in the two examples are worked out from the same rating functions as the company pages, at the close of Fri 13 Nov 2026.
