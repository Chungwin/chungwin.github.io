How it works

Five dials, one for the US stock market and one for each metal's junior miners. Each dial has three gauges and the same five levels. The dials are never added up: each one answers its own question and controls its own thing.

One dial

US market

Question: would a stock-market decline turn into forced selling?

Controls: how much cash to hold, and the total size of the risky pile (developers and explorers, all metals together).

Three gauges:

  • S&P 500 earnings yield vs the 10-year US Treasury
  • US high-yield credit spreads
  • FINRA margin debt (growth, and share of GDP)

Why the S&P 500 and not the Nasdaq: the question is whether a decline spreads to everything, which is about the broad market, not tech. A tech bust alone did not hurt gold miners in 2000–02; a squeeze in credit and leverage did in 2008, 2020 and 2022. Why the US: liquidity crises have started in or spread through US markets, and most buyers of miners trade in New York and Toronto.

One dial per metal

Junior miners: gold/silver · copper · uranium · rare earths

Question: are this metal's small mining companies in a frenzy? (The metal price itself is not a gauge.)

Controls: whether that metal's corner of the pile is open or closed for buying, and which corner is cut first.

Three gauges:

  • Is it easy for small companies to raise money?
  • Are the stocks expensive?
  • Is everyone piling in?

What the dials never touch

Physical gold and silver, and the producing miners. Those are governed by the owner's separate rules for individual holdings (the thesis, see glossary). Any single company is still bought or sold by its own rules; the dials only size the pile and say which corners of it are open.

Cash
Cash means cash. It is the ammunition, and inflation is the price of keeping that option. Gold does not count toward the cash bucket.
Funded
Cash plus committed funding covers at least 18 months of burn, including the cost of the next catalyst, with no raise required.
Trim
At metal level Overheated, trim only the part of a position above its ceiling, never the position. A 3× is the first leg of a 10×.
Tax order
default Selling is the last resort, and it goes in tax order. A hotter dial first closes the buy window and lets new money build cash; no sales for rebalancing at Elevated. Sales start at Worrisome, after a confirmed reading, from the metal with the hottest own dial: positions at a loss first, then the smallest gains, then the winners. A realised loss offsets gains in the same year, so the first sales are often tax-free on balance. Physical gold held over a year is tax-free in Germany; miner gains are not. Confirm the details with a tax adviser.

How to read a gauge

US market gauges
S&P vs bonds
Hot when the index's forward earnings yield sits at or below the 10-year Treasury yield: stocks then only win if earnings forecasts hold. Cold when the earnings yield is well above the bond yield.
Credit
The ICE BofA US High Yield option-adjusted spread (FRED, daily): the extra yield junk-rated US companies pay over Treasuries. Hot below ~300 bp (no fear priced). Cold above ~500 bp. Crash signal above ~600 bp.
Margin debt
The fuel for forced selling. Hot when FINRA margin debt is up more than ~30% year on year, or at a record share of GDP or market cap. Cold when down more than ~15% year on year. Growth is the main reading; the share is the sanity check. Margin-debt growth peaked a few months before the 2000, 2007 and 2021 tops.
Metal gauges
Raise money
The yardstick is the TSX Venture Exchange's monthly financing statistics (TMX Group), the market where most juniors raise, plus per-metal deal news. Four hot markers: money raised over twelve months at or above the 2021 record (C$11.0 bn); the number of financings back at the 2021 pace (1,684 a year); placements at a premium becoming common; a rush of new listings. Hot when two or more are lit. Cold when twelve-month money raised is down more than 40% on a year earlier (as in 2022), with raises small, discounted and hard to fill. The most important question: an open window means dilution at the top, a closed one means no money at the bottom.
Expensive
Hot when producers trade well above net asset value (above ~1.2x) or small companies jump on announcements with nothing new in them. Cold when producers sit below 0.8x and developers below 0.5x.
Piling in
Hot when retail and generalist funds flood in, every newsletter is bullish, insiders sell into strength. Cold when the sector is ignored and money is leaving its ETFs.
Instruments
S&P Global gold and base-metals financings; the Sprott Physical Uranium Trust's premium or discount to NAV; rare-earth placements at a premium and new listings; GDXJ, URNJ and REMX flows. A new metal gets its own three questions and its own row. Nothing else changes.

How it moves

The dials are deliberately slow. Nobody can time markets, so the playbook accepts being a little late in exchange for not being whipsawed. Four things are held to even when they feel wrong; the rest is defaults.

Four commitments

Most of this page is a set of defaults, meant to guide judgment rather than replace it. Deviate when you have a good reason, and note it in the log. These four are the exception: they're kept even when they feel wrong, because that's exactly when they matter.

  1. Always keep some cash. Never drop below the cash level of the current set level, and never below 10%. Guards against feeling certain.
  2. Let the ladder run. Once a crash signal has fired, buy at each step as it's reached. The steps themselves can be adjusted, but only in calm markets. Guards against fear.
  3. Don't add to what's running hot. No new money for a metal at Elevated or above. Guards against chasing.
  4. Move in two halves. When a dial turns hotter, adjust new money at once, but sell only after a second monthly reading agrees. When it turns colder, wait for two readings. Guards against overreacting.

The rhythm

Every trading day · automatic

Crash check

Crashes can happen in days, so three numbers are checked the morning after every US trading day:

  • S&P 500: how far below its high
  • US credit spreads
  • Gold miners (GDX): how far below their high

Act only if the crash signal fires or a ladder step is hit. A step counts once GDX closes at that level, even if it bounces back the next day. Otherwise nothing changes.

Monthly

Full reading

Read all gauges on all five dials and write them in the log, together with any deviation from a default and its reason.

Turning hotter: new money follows at once; any selling waits for a second month that agrees. Turning colder: two months in a row. A dial moves one level at a time.

Once a year

Review

Did the dials protect anything, or only cost return? Compare with doing nothing.

If after two years they only cost return, loosen them. Also check what holding cash really costs.

When a crash comes

  1. 1
    The signal fires

    S&P 500 down 20% from its high and US credit spreads above 600 bp. Both are needed: a fall in stocks alone can be an ordinary correction. With credit seizing too, it is the kind of crash that drags miners down and creates the buying chance. The signal says what kind of fall it is; the ladder below decides when to buy.

  2. 2
    The US market dial jumps to Fear

    Straight away, without waiting for a second month.

  3. 3
    Cash is spent in steps as miners fall steps are defaults

    One third of the cash each time gold miners close 35%, 55% and 70% below their highest close of the last two years. The money goes into junior miners, picked by research, in the metals whose dials read lowest. GDX only measures how deep the fall is; it is not what gets bought. Letting the ladder run is one of the four commitments; the levels, the equal thirds and the index are defaults, adjustable in calm markets, never while a signal is active.

    "Gold miners" = GDX, the VanEck Gold Miners ETF: about 65 of the largest gold and silver producers and royalty companies (Newmont, Agnico Eagle, Barrick…), the most traded miner fund, with daily prices back to 2006.

About

Where it comes from

Howard Marks of Oaktree introduced INVESTCON in his memo The Calculus of Value (August 2025). He modelled it on the Pentagon's DEFCON scale, where 5 is peacetime and 1 is nuclear war: the lower the number, the more alarmed you are. His six stages, in his own order and words:

MarksHis actionHere
—No resting state in Marks' scale1Fear · 2Normal
INVESTCON 6Stop buying3Elevated no new money to juniors
INVESTCON 5Reduce aggressive holdings and increase defensive holdings4Worrisome pile to 20%, cash to 25%
INVESTCON 4Sell off the remaining aggressive holdings5Overheated pile to 15%, cash to 30–35%
INVESTCON 3Trim defensive holdings as wellNot used: producers and metal are never sold by a dial
INVESTCON 2Eliminate all holdingsNot used: the book is long-only
INVESTCON 1Go shortNot used

In the memo Marks wrote that it is "essentially impossible to reasonably reach the degree of certainty needed to implement INVESTCON 3, 2, or 1," but that he had "no problem thinking it's time for INVESTCON 5." His reasons were the S&P 500's elevated P/E, the relationship between Treasury yields and the index's dividend yield, and general investor exuberance. The first two are the ancestors of this page's market gauges.

Four things are different here:

  • The numbers run the other way. 1 to 5 rising with the heat, so a newcomer can read the dial like a thermometer rather than a military alert.
  • The scale is shifted. Marks' scale starts at caution ("stop buying"); this page adds two levels below it, a resting state and a buying state. At the other end it stops at his stage 4: long-term holdings are never trimmed by a dial, and the book never goes to zero or short.
  • Less is dialled. Marks moves a whole portfolio between aggressive and defensive. This page only sizes cash and the pile of small companies that need outside money; the long-term holdings stay put.
  • Nothing is compiled. A market dial for the liquidity risk that hits everything at once, and one dial per metal for the cycles that run separately. Earlier drafts merged them into one number, and each merge needed a rule whose only job was to combine things that don't belong together. Kept apart, those rules disappear, and the structure survives adding metals or markets.

Why only the small companies

The dials govern developers and explorers because a crash hits them twice: the price falls, then the funding window shuts, and a raise at the bottom is permanent per-share damage. Gold recovers first; producers fall hard but keep their cash flow. A crash is treated as a reset to Fear rather than as a level of its own.

Rules or principles?

Mostly principles. A rule earns its place only where judgment is predictably worst: when afraid, and when greedy. Four commitments are kept for that reason; the rest are defaults that may be overridden with a reason noted in the log, which the yearly review then looks back on. The thresholds come from four historical episodes and reasoning, not a backtest, so treating them as fixed would be false precision. Some may earn promotion after two reviews.

What it is not

The dials never override the thesis: its kill-switches decide whether an investment case is still alive, the dials decide how much illiquid exposure to carry while it is. The rules do not depend on current holdings. The cash and pile percentages are judgment, not data, and the yearly review is where they get checked.

Glossary

Risky pile
Developers (companies building a mine, not yet producing) and explorers (companies still looking), across all metals. The part of the book that needs outside money to survive.
Juniors
Same thing, the industry's word for small mining companies without production.
P/NAV
Price to net asset value. What the market pays for a miner relative to what its ounces are worth on paper. Below 1.0x is a discount; the 2011 bull peak saw 1.2–1.5x.
Credit spreads (high-yield spread)
The extra interest that US companies with a junk rating (BB and lower) pay to borrow, compared with the US government, in basis points (100 bp = 1 percentage point). The page uses the ICE BofA US High Yield Index option-adjusted spread, about 2,000 bonds, published daily by the St. Louis Fed (FRED series BAMLH0A0HYM2, one day behind). Junk bonds are used because the weakest borrowers lose access to money first, so their spread is the earliest sign of fear. Low spreads mean nobody fears defaults; above 600 bp means credit is seizing.
Earnings yield
Earnings divided by price, the inverse of the P/E ratio. Comparing it with the bond yield shows how much extra you are paid for owning stocks instead of Treasuries.
Margin debt
Money borrowed from brokers to buy stocks, reported monthly by FINRA. When prices fall, brokers demand repayment and the borrowed stocks get sold: forced selling.
Drawdown
How far an index has fallen from its highest point.
Funded
A company that can reach its next milestone without raising money: 18 months of spending covered, including the milestone's own cost.
Position ceiling
The largest share of the book one company is allowed to be. Set in the thesis, not here.
The thesis
The owner's written investment case and the rules for individual holdings: why each is owned, what would prove it wrong (the kill-switches), and how big it may be. This page sits beside it and never overrides it.
Crash signal
S&P 500 down 20% from its high and high-yield spreads above 600 bp, together. Resets the US market dial to Fear and starts the ladder.
Ladder
The plan for spending cash in a crash: a third each time gold miners (GDX) close 35%, 55% and 70% below their highest close of the last two years.
GDX
The VanEck Gold Miners ETF: about 65 of the largest gold and silver miners and royalty companies (Newmont, Agnico Eagle, Barrick, Wheaton, Franco-Nevada…). The page uses it as the yardstick for how far miners have fallen.
